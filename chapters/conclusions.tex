We have developed a Bayesian framework for statistical inference with functional data based on \acs{RKHSs}. The main idea has been to exploit the close relationship between a stochastic process and the \acs{RKHS} generated by its covariance function, in order to construct functional models that are both mathematically tractable and computationally feasible. By representing the functional parameter as a finite linear combination of kernel sections, we obtain models that retain a functional interpretation while reducing the difficult problem of Bayesian inference in an infinite-dimensional space to a variable-dimensional parametric problem.

Two fundamental statistical problems have been considered within this framework. The first is functional regression, for which Bayesian linear and logistic models have been proposed, together with prediction strategies and variable-selection procedures. The second is functional hypothesis testing, where a Bayesian one-sample test for the mean of a Gaussian process has been constructed using a spike-and-slab prior. This methodology provides not only a decision concerning the null hypothesis, but also posterior measures of the evidence against it and direct uncertainty quantification for the underlying functional effect.

The Bayesian aspect of the model serves different purposes. First, it provides a simple inference technique in the \acs{RKHS} models with increased flexibility, applicable to a wide range of prior distributions and generally appealing to practitioners. Second, it increases the interpretability of the resulting decisions, since we have access to a whole posterior distribution that explains the influence of each parameter, as well as their interactions. Third, it naturally allows quantifying uncertainty in the process, providing additional information in the form of posterior summaries or credible bands.

An important part of the thesis has been to establish theoretical foundations for these Bayesian procedures. For functional regression, posterior consistency has been obtained in the presence of an unknown number of components, together with posterior contraction results based on both Doob's theorem and Schwartz's approach. Similar analyses for other functional regression models were carried out for example by \citet{lian2016posterior} or \citet{abraham2020posterior}, albeit not within a \acs{RKHS} framework. For functional testing, posterior consistency of the Bayes factor in a point-null testing situation was established by \citet{dass2004note}, but their abstract methodology cannot yield posterior contraction rates. Thanks to the \acs{RKHS} approach, we have successfully provided contraction rates under the null and alternative hypotheses. In particular, the asymmetry between polynomial concentration under the null and exponential concentration under fixed alternatives has been characterized, and the exponential contraction rate has been shown to be arbitrarily close to the optimal rate. These results provide a frequentist justification for the Bayesian procedures while also clarifying the asymptotic behavior of the evidence accumulated by the models.

Furthermore, the trans-dimensional aspect of the posterior approximation is arguably a novel contribution in functional data. By combining the proposed \acs{RKHS} representation with \acs{RJMCMC}, the complexity of the models can be inferred jointly with their parameters, without requiring a separate model selection step. This approach is in line with recent trends in Bayesian methods, where increased computational capability allows the practical use of more time-consuming approximation techniques. The performance is satisfactory in the numerical experiments, even with the relatively small number of components we selected. In the regression setting, the \acs{RKHS} methods are on a par with a broad collection of functional and finite-dimensional alternatives in terms of predictive performance. In the testing setting, the proposed test shows an accurate control of the type I error and decent power across a range of scenarios.

Overall, we have developed a unified, interpretable, and theoretically grounded Bayesian approach to functional inference, combining \acs{RKHS} representations, trans-dimensional posterior computation, uncertainty quantification, and rigorous asymptotic guarantees. The proposed models and methods are not meant to replace existing techniques, but rather to complement them by providing alternatives with a different balance between flexibility, interpretability, computational tractability, and theoretical background.

\section{Future work and open research lines}

We believe that this Bayesian framework opens several compelling paths for future research, demonstrating the practical and theoretical utility of \acs{RKHS} representations in managing the inherent infinite-dimensionality of functional data. We give a few examples of possible future research directions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Studying the role of impact points within the \acs{RKHS} approximation. This could yield meaningful new methodologies for change-point detection or variable selection in functional estimation.
    \item Completing the theoretical analysis of the functional regression models by explicitly establishing results in the logistic case. They should be similar to the results for the linear case, but the proofs might be more involved and require different techniques that provide insights into the \acs{RKHS} formulation.
    \item Considering noisy observations \(\tilde X_i = X_i + \epsilon_i\) with \(\epsilon_i \sim \gp(0, K_*)\) independent from \(X\), which is often the case in real applications.
    \item Extending the testing framework to non-Gaussian data. For example, by means of mixtures of Gaussians or a more general distribution. 
    \item Considering two-sample tests to assess equality of means in two different functional populations. Both the homoscedastic and heteroscedastic cases are of interest.
    \item Exploring non-local alternatives in the sense of \citet{johnson2010use}, which could provide exponential contraction rates under the null and alternative hypotheses. For example, \citet{rockova2018bayesian} proposes a continuous spike-and-slab prior, where the prior on the null hypothesis is concentrated around \(b=0\), but is not a Dirac delta.
    \item From a computational perspective, there is ample room to improve the proposal mechanisms for the birth stage in \acs{RJMCMC}.
\end{itemize}
