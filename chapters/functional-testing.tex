\noindent Another goal of this thesis is to study Bayesian approaches to functional hypothesis testing. Despite their broad adoption elsewhere in modern statistics, Bayesian methods remain comparatively underdeveloped within the \acs{FDA} framework, particularly for testing problems. We aim to bridge this gap by proposing a Bayesian test for the mean function \(\mu\) of a Gaussian process observed through i.i.d.\ samples, which serves as a building block for more complex statistical analyses. In particular, we provide a method for testing the point-null hypothesis \(H_0: \mu \equiv \mu_0\) versus the composite alternative \(H_1: \mu \not\equiv \mu_0\), where \(\mu_0(\cdot)\) is a predetermined function. We focus on the simple hypothesis of exact equality, in contrast to \textit{precise} or \textit{relevant} hypotheses such as \(H_0^\epsilon: \|\mu-\mu_0\| < \epsilon\) for a given \(\epsilon>0\). Although these precise hypothesis may be useful when an exact zero is not meaningful or reasonable, in a Bayesian framework they can be conceptually encoded in the corresponding point-null hypothesis via prior beliefs, as argued by \citet{berger1987testing}. The proposed test is based on the so-called \textit{Bayes factor}, which is defined as
\[
\bff_{10} = \frac{\pp(\text{data}\mid H_1)}{\pp(\text{data}\mid H_0)}
\]
and measures the evidence for \(H_1\) over \(H_0\). It is linked to the prior and posterior odds through the formula
\[
\frac{\pp(H_1\mid \text{data})}{\pp(H_0\mid \text{data})} = \bff_{10} \, \times\, \frac{\pp(H_1)}{\pp(H_0)}.
\]
Using Bayes factors to quantify the evidence against the null hypothesis in a continuous manner is the standard approach in multivariate Bayesian testing, popularized since the seminal work by \citet{kass1995bayes}. Because they are intimately related to the posterior probability of the null, Bayes factors provide a direct probabilistic way of interpreting the test decision, while also allowing the incorporation of prior information. Moreover, despite being a loose analog of a likelihood ratio test, the Bayes factor \(\bff_{10}\) can be inverted to provide evidence in favor of \(H_0\), which is not possible in frequentist tests due to the built-in asymmetry. 

\section{Reproducing kernel model for functional testing}\label{sec:rkhs-test}

Most functional tests in the literature employ some form of dimension reduction via projections or basis expansions as a first step. We take a slightly different approach and directly assume, as we did in Chapter~\ref{ch:functional-regression}, that the mean function is a member of \acs{RKHS} associated with the covariance function of the underlying process, so that it can be approximated through a finite linear combination of kernel sections. For simplicity, in this chapter we restrict ourselves to Gaussian data.

Let \(X_{1:n}=(X_1,\dots, X_n)\) be an i.i.d.\ sample of a Gaussian process \(X\sim \gp(\mu, K)\) with mean \(\mu\) and strictly positive definite covariance function \(K\), which is assumed to be known for the time being. We will mainly work with functional observations, but whenever discretization is needed we will suppose that the process is observed on an arbitrarily dense grid \(\mathcal S=\{s_1,\dots, s_G\}\subset [0,1]\) of size \(G\). Moreover, discretized quantities on the grid will be denoted by boldface letters. Without loss of generality we set \(\mu_0\equiv 0\) and consider the hypotheses
\[
H_0: \mu(t)=0 \text{ for all } t \in [0,1] \quad \text{vs.} \quad H_1: \mu(t)\neq 0 \text{ for some } t \in [0,1].
\]

Our key modeling assumption is that \(\mu\in\Hcal(K)\). As we already established, not only is this space naturally linked to the process \(X\), but in general it contains smoother functions than, say, \(L^2[0,1]\). More importantly, it provides a direct finite-dimensional representation of the mean function if we further suppose that \(\mu\in\Hcal_0(K)\), which is dense in \(\Hcal(K)\) by construction. Indeed, we propose a parametric model (with a functional interpretation) based on the approximation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mu-h0}
\mu_{\theta_p}(\cdot) = \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j K(t_j, \cdot),
\end{equation}
where \(\theta_p=(p, \tau_p, b_p) \equiv (p, t_1,\dots, t_p, \beta_1,\dots,\beta_p)\) is a generic parameter. As always, we will omit the subscript \(p\) when it is understood from context, and assume that the grid of observation \(\mathcal S\) is fine enough to contain all the relevant time instants.

Note that when \(K\) is strictly positive definite, the null hypothesis \(\mu_\theta\equiv 0\) corresponds to \(b=0\) as long as the time instants are distinct. Thus, the parameter spaces for the null and alternative hypotheses in our Bayesian test are
\[
\Theta_0=\bigcup_{p\in\N}\left\{b \in \R^p: b=0\right\}
\]
and
\[
  \Theta_1=\bigcup_{p\in\N}\left\{(p, \tau, b): \tau \in \mathcal T_p,\, b \in \R^p\setminus \{0\}\right\},
\]
respectively, where \(\mathcal T_p = \{(t_1,\dots,t_p)\in [0,1]^p: t_1 < \cdots < t_p\}\) is the ordered unit simplex. Although not strictly needed, we impose an ordering constraint to avoid identifiability issues and reduce the parameter space by a factor of \(p!\) on each dimension.

\subsection{Likelihood and prior distribution} 

Let \(\pp_\mu\) be the distribution of \(\gp(\mu, K)\) on the path space \(\mathcal C[0,1]\), and similarly let \(\pp_0\) be the distribution of \(\gp(0, K)\). Since both distributions are Gaussian with common covariance, the Feldman--Hájek dichotomy \citep{feldman1958equivalence} implies that \(\pp_\mu\) is absolutely continuous with respect to \(\pp_0\) whenever \(\mu\in\Hcal(K)\), which is precisely our assumption on the mean function. In this situation, the density of \(\pp_{\mu_\theta}\) with respect to \(\pp_0\) is given by \citep[Theorem 1]{berrendero2018use}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rn-derivative}
  \frac{d\pp_{\mu_\theta}}{d\pp_0}(X) = \exp\left\{\langle X, \mu_\theta\rangle_K - \frac{1}{2}\|\mu_\theta\|^2_K\right\}.
\end{equation}
In a Bayesian setting, this Radon--Nikodym derivative can be interpreted as the likelihood \(L(X|\theta)\) of observing a path \(X\) under the parameter \(\mu_{\theta}\), with respect to the common reference measure \(\pp_0\). Now, since \(\mu_{\theta}\) has the finite-dimensional representation~\eqref{eq:mu-h0}, we can evaluate the scalar product (via Loève's isometry) and the \acs{RKHS} norm, obtaining closed-form expressions 
\[
  \langle X, \mu_\theta\rangle_K = \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j X(t_j)\quad \text{and}\quad \|\mu_\theta\|^2_K = \sum_{j=1}^p \sum_{k=1}^p \beta_j\beta_k K(t_j, t_k).
\] 
In particular, for our i.i.d.\ sample the joint likelihood becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:likelihood}
  L(X_{1:n}\mid p, \tau, b) = \exp\left\{S_n(\tau)'b - \frac{n}{2}b' K_{\tau} b\right\},
\end{equation}
where \(S_n(\tau)=\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i(t_1), \dots, X_i(t_p))'\) and \(K_{\tau}=[K(t_j, t_k)]_{j,k=1}^p\). 

Then, for a random variable \(\theta=(p,\tau,b)\) we consider the parametric hierarchical model
\begin{equation}\label{eq:model-testing}
  \begin{split}
  p &\sim \pi,\\
  \tau\mid p &\sim \mathfrak{T}_p,\\
  b\mid p &\sim \mathfrak{B}_p,\\
  X_{1:n} \mid \theta &\sim \gp(\mu_\theta, K) \quad \text{i.i.d.},
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
where \(\pi\), \(\mathfrak{T}_p\) and \(\mathfrak{B}_p\) are probability measures on \(\N\), \(\mathcal T_p\) and \(\R^p\), respectively.\footnote{The implied independence between \(\tau\) and \(b\) (given \(p\)) is just a convenience, and not essential to the model.} We denote by \(\Pi\) the prior distribution on \(\theta\) implied by the model in~\eqref{eq:model-testing}. The prior on the time instants can be chosen freely (e.g.\ uniform on \(\mathcal T_p\)), but the prior on the coefficient vector \(b\) has to be selected carefully, since any continuous distribution will give zero mass to the null event \(\{b=0\}\). To remedy this, we follow a \textit{spike-and-slab} approach reminiscent of Bayesian variable selection in linear regression \citep[e.g.][]{mitchell1988bayesian,ishwaran2005spike}, whereby a fixed mass is assigned to the null hypothesis and the remaining mass is distributed continuously on the alternative. Let \(0<\pi_0<1\) be the prior belief that the null hypothesis is true, and consider
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prior-b}
  \begin{split}
\Pi(b\mid p) &= \pi_0 \delta_0(b) + (1-\pi_0)\mathcal N_p(b\mid 0, \eta^2 I_p)\\
  &= \pi_0\Pi^{\text{spike}}(b) + (1-\pi_0)\Pi^{\text{slab}}(b),
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
where \(\delta_0\) is a Dirac measure at \(0\in \R^p\) (the ``spike''), \(\eta^2>0\) is a scalar hyperparameter, and \(I_p\) is the identity matrix of order \(p\). The mixture notation is a shorthand for a hierarchical representation with a latent indicator \(Z\sim \text{Bernoulli}(\pi_0)\) and conditional distributions 
\[
  b\mid Z=1, p \sim \delta_0\quad \text{and}\quad b\mid Z=0, p \sim \mathcal N_p(0, \eta^2 I_p).
\]
The choice of a normal distribution for the ``slab'' prior of \(b\) is not arbitrary; it will be used as a conjugacy device to simplify the posterior distribution later on. Note that this construction induces the priors \(\Pi(H_0)=\pi_0\) and \(\Pi(H_1)=1-\pi_0\), since the null hypothesis is reinterpreted as \(H_0: b=0\).

\subsection{Posterior approximation}\label{sec:posterior-approximation}

Bayes' formula readily gives a posterior distribution 
\[
  \Pi_n(p, \tau, b \mid X_{1:n}) \propto \Pi(p, \tau, b)L(X_{1:n}\mid p, \tau, b)
\] 
up to a multiplicative constant, which can be approximated through computational methods. However, sampling the high-dimensional parameter \(b\in \R^p\) is notoriously challenging, so we marginalize it analytically thanks to the prior structure in~\eqref{eq:prior-b}. The marginal likelihood has the form
\begin{align*}
  m(X_{1:n}\mid p, \tau) &= \pi_0\int \Pi^{\text{spike}}(b)L(X_{1:n}\mid p, \tau, b)\, db\\
  &\quad + (1-\pi_0)\int \Pi^{\text{slab}}(b)L(X_{1:n}\mid p, \tau, b)\, db\\
  &= \pi_0 m_{\text{spike}}(X_{1:n}\mid p, \tau) + (1-\pi_0)\mslab(X_{1:n}\mid p, \tau).
\end{align*}
According to~\eqref{eq:likelihood} we immediately get \(m_{\text{spike}}(X_{1:n}|p,\tau)=e^0=1\), since \(\Pi^{\text{spike}}(b)\) is a point mass at \(b=0\). In the slab part we get an exact integral, applying the formula for the moment generating function (at \(t=1\)) of a quadratic form in Gaussian variables \citep[Corollary 3.2a.1]{mathai1992quadratic}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:m-slab}
  \begin{split}
  \mslab(X_{1:n}\mid p, \tau) &= \int \mathcal N_p(b\mid 0, \eta^2 I_p)L(X_{1:n}\mid p, \tau, b)\, db \\
  &= \eta^{-p} |\Lambda_\tau|^{-1/2}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{2}S_n(\tau)' \Lambda_\tau^{-1}S_n(\tau)\right\},
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
where \(\Lambda_\tau = nK_\tau + \eta^{-2}I_p\). All in all, the marginal posterior expression becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:posterior-p-tau}
\Pi_n(p, \tau\mid X_{1:n}) \propto \Pi(p)\Pi(\tau\mid p)[\pi_0 + (1-\pi_0)\mslab(X_{1:n}\mid p, \tau)].
\end{equation}

At this point we again use the \acs{RJMCMC} algorithm (see Chapter~\ref{ch:rjmcmc}) to approximate the marginal posterior \(\Pi_n(p, \tau| X_{1:n})\). This method yields a sequence of approximate posterior draws \(\{(p^{(r)}, \tau^{(r)}): r=1,\dots,R\}\), possibly of different dimensions. We can subsequently reapply Bayes' formula conditional on \((p^{(r)},\tau^{(r)})\) to recover the posterior samples \(b^{(r)}\), which in turn can be used to construct posterior samples \(\mu^{(r)}\) of the mean function via~\eqref{eq:mu-h0}. To that effect, consider the conditional posterior probability of the spike 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:psi-posterior-spike}
\psi_n(p,\tau) = \Pi_n(b=0\mid p,\tau, X_{1:n}) = \frac{\pi_0}{m(X_{1:n}\mid p,\tau)},
\end{equation}
and the conditional posterior distribution of the coefficients
\[
\Pi_n(b\mid p,\tau, X_{1:n}) = \psi_n(p, \tau)\delta_0(b) + (1-\psi_n(p, \tau))\Pi_n^{\text{slab}}(b\mid p,\tau, X_{1:n}),
\]
both computed through Bayes' formula. For the slab part, we have 
\begin{align*}
  \hspace*{-1.4em}\Pi_n^{\text{slab}}(b\mid p,\tau, X_{1:n}) &\propto \Pi^{\text{slab}}(b)L(X_{1:n}\mid p, \tau, b)\\
  &\propto \mathcal N_p(b\mid 0, \eta^2 I_p) \exp\left\{S_n(\tau)'b - \frac{n}{2}b' K_{\tau} b\right\}\\
  &\propto \exp\left\{\frac{-1}{2}(b - \Lambda_\tau^{-1}S_n(\tau))'\Lambda_\tau(b -  \Lambda_\tau^{-1}S_n(\tau)) \right\}.
\end{align*}
Hence, we can conclude that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mean-b-slab}
  b \mid p,\tau, X_{1:n}, \text{slab} \sim \mathcal N_p(\Lambda_\tau^{-1}S_n(\tau), \Lambda_\tau^{-1}).
\end{equation} 
Thus, after a full \acs{RJMCMC} run with \(R\) posterior samples \((p^{(r)}, \tau^{(r)})\), we can retrieve the corresponding posterior samples \(b^{(r)}\) through the following simple procedure:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Draw a spike or slab event \(Z^{(r)} \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\psi_n(p^{(r)}, \tau^{(r)}))\).
  \item If \(Z^{(r)}=1\), set \(b^{(r)}=0\). If \(Z^{(r)}=0\), sample \(b^{(r)}\) from the distribution \(\mathcal N_{p^{(r)}}(\Lambda_{\tau^{(r)}}^{-1}S_n(\tau^{(r)}), \Lambda_{\tau^{(r)}}^{-1})\).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Decision criteria} 

One of the main advantages of our Bayesian framework is that it naturally provides several posterior metrics to evaluate the evidence against the null hypothesis given the data. Below we analyze several of these metrics.


\paragraph{Posterior null probability} As we said before, the posterior probability of the null \(\Pi_n(b=0|X_{1:n})\) corresponds to \(\E[\mathds{1}\{b=0\}|X_{1:n}]\). We could naively compute the estimator \(R^{-1}\sum_{r} \mathds{1}\{b^{(r)}=0\}\) with our posterior samples, which by the ergodic properties of the \acs{RJMCMC} sampler would indeed converge almost surely to the target probability (under standard assumptions). However, this estimator introduces the Monte Carlo noise of actually sampling \(b^{(r)}\), which is not desirable. Instead, we can use the equivalence
\begin{align*}
  \E[\psi_n(p,\tau)\mid X_{1:n}] &= \sum_p \int_{\mathcal T_p} \psi_n(p,\tau) \Pi_n(p,\tau\mid X_{1:n})\, d\tau\\
  &= \Pi_n(b=0\mid X_{1:n})
\end{align*}
to obtain a more efficient estimator of the posterior probability of \(H_0\). Indeed, by the law of total variance we have:
\begin{align*}
  \var(\psi_n(p,\tau)\mid X_{1:n}) &= \var(\E[\mathds{1}\{b=0\}\mid p, \tau, X_{1:n}]\mid X_{1:n})\\
  &\leq \var(\mathds{1}\{b=0\}\mid X_{1:n}).
\end{align*}
Hence, conditioning on \((p,\tau)\) and averaging the posterior spike probabilities \(\psi_n(p^{(r)}, \tau^{(r)})\) instead of the raw indicators \(\mathds{1}\{b^{(r)}=0\}\) yields the improved estimator
\[
\hat \pi_{\text{null}} = \frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^R \psi_n(p^{(r)}, \tau^{(r)}).
\]
Note that this strategy is similar to the Rao--Blackwellization process to reduce the variance of an estimator \citep[Theorem 7.9]{ghosh2006introduction}. Under the \(0\)--\(1\) loss function, we would reject the null hypothesis if \(\hat \pi_{\text{null}} \leq 1/2\) and accept it otherwise, but practitioners can fix any arbitrary threshold on a case-by-case basis.

\paragraph{Bayes factor} Another quantity of interest to assess the evidence against the null hypothesis is the Bayes factor, which is defined as
\[
\bff_{10}(X_{1:n}) = \frac{L(X_{1:n}\mid H_1)}{L(X_{1:n}\mid H_0)} = \frac{1 - \Pi_n(H_0\mid X_{1:n})}{\Pi_n(H_0\mid X_{1:n})} \cdot \frac{\pi_0}{1-\pi_0}
\]
and represents the evidence in favor of \(H_1\) over \(H_0\) as supported by the data. For example, if \(\pi_0=1-\pi_0=1/2\), then \(\Pi_n(H_0|X_{1:n})=1/2\) corresponds to \(\bff_{10}(X_{1:n}) = 1\). Note that in our model \(L(X_{1:n}|H_0)\) is actually equal to 1 by construction, since we take \(\pp_0\) as the reference measure. In practice, we report an estimated Bayes factor 
\[
\hat{{\bff}}_{10} = \frac{1 - \hat \pi_{\text{null}}}{\hat \pi_{\text{null}}}\cdot \frac{\pi_0}{1-\pi_0}
\]
and use Jeffreys' scale (see Table~\ref{tab:jeffreys-scale}) to weigh the evidence against \(H_0\). Observe that the Bayes factor (a likelihood ratio) is actually independent of the prior belief \(\pi_0\), so it acts as a somewhat more objective measure to compare evidence against \(H_0\) across different models.

\paragraph{Region of practical equivalence} Lastly, we can report the posterior probability of the so-called \acf{ROPE}, defined for a small \(\epsilon > 0\) as 
\[
\Pi_n\left(\sup_s |\mu(s)| < \epsilon \mathrel{\big\vert} X_{1:n}\right).
\]
This ``practical null'' probability is sometimes used to evaluate \(H_0\) when an exact zero is not meaningful, and is readily available in our Bayesian setting without changing the overall approach (i.e.\ without considering a precise hypothesis \(H_0^\epsilon:\sup_s|\mu(s)| < \epsilon\) from the start). The easiest way to approximate the \acs{ROPE} probability on the observation grid \(\mathcal S\) is through the Monte Carlo estimator
\[
\hat\pi_{\text{ROPE}}(\epsilon) = \frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^R \mathds{1}\left\{\max_{s\in\mathcal S} |\mu^{(r)}(s)| < \epsilon\right\}.
\]

\subsection{Posterior summaries and uncertainty quantification}\label{sec:posterior-summaries}

An additional benefit of the Bayesian framework is that it facilitates posterior inference on the mean function as a by-product of the testing procedure. For example, we can estimate the posterior mean function \(\mu^{\text{PM}}(\cdot)=\E[\mu(\cdot)|X]\) using the conditional expectation strategy again, which is typically much less noisy than averaging posterior draws \(\mu^{(r)}\) directly. Indeed, for a fixed \(s\in[0,1]\) the law of total expectation gives 
\[
  \E[\mu(s)\mid X] = \E[\E[\mu(s)\mid p, \tau, X]\mid X],
\]
and the same variance reduction argument as before applies. In the i.i.d.\ case, from the computations in Section~\ref{sec:posterior-approximation}, the inner posterior expectation admits the closed-form expression 
\begin{align*}
\E[\mu(s)\mid p, \tau, X_{1:n}] &= \sum_{j=1}^p \E[\beta_j\mid p, \tau, X_{1:n}]K(t_j, s) \\
&=(1-\psi_n(p,\tau))K(\tau, s)'\E[b\mid p, \tau, X_{1:n}, \text{slab}]\\
&= (1-\psi_n(p, \tau))K(\tau, s)'\Lambda_\tau^{-1}S_n(\tau),
\end{align*}
where \(K(\tau, s)\) denotes the \(p\times 1\) vector with entries \(K(t_j, s)\). Consequently, a Monte Carlo estimator \(\hat\mu_n^{\text{PM}}(\cdot) = \hat{\E}[\mu(\cdot)|X_{1:n}]\) of the posterior mean function is given by
\[
\hat\mu_n^{\text{PM}}(\cdot) =\frac{1}{R} \sum_{r=1}^R (1-\psi_n(p^{(r)}, \tau^{(r)}))K(\tau^{(r)}, \cdot)'\Lambda_{\tau^{(r)}}^{-1}S_n(\tau^{(r)}),
\]
which is then evaluated on the grid \(\mathcal S\). Note that this can be seen as a regularized estimator for \(\mu\) in which a certain weight \(\pi_0\) was placed on it being the zero function.

Along with the posterior expectation of \(\mu\), it is common to report credible bands around it to quantify the uncertainty in the posterior estimation. In particular, for any \(\alpha\in(0,1)\) we compute simultaneous \(1-\alpha\) credible bands, that is, bands that contain the entire function \(\mu\) with posterior probability \(1-\alpha\). The usual approach for constructing such bands is the maximum deviation method described by \citet{crainiceanu2007spatially}. The main idea is to standardize the deviations from the posterior mean by the pointwise posterior standard deviation 
\[
  \sigma(s)=\sqrt{\var(\mu(s)\mid X_{1:n})},
\]
to obtain bands that adapt to the local uncertainty. In particular, we aim to find a single critical value \(q_{1-\alpha}\) satisfying
\[
\Pi_n\left(\sup_{s\in [0,1]}\, \frac{|\mu(s) - \mu^{\text{PM}}(s)|}{\sigma(s)} \leq q_{1-\alpha} \mathrel{\big\vert} X_{1:n}\right)= 1-\alpha,
\]
and then form symmetrical bands \(\{\mu^{\text{PM}}(s) \pm q_{1-\alpha}\sigma(s): s\in [0,1]\}\). In practice, this procedure is carried out on the discretized grid. First, compute the pointwise posterior standard deviation at each grid point \(s\in\mathcal S\) as 
\[
\hat\sigma(s)=\sqrt{\var(\{\mu^{(r)}(s)\}_{r=1}^R)}.
\]
Then, for each draw compute the standardized supremum deviation
\[
D^{(r)} = \max_{s\in\mathcal S} \frac{|\mu^{(r)}(s) - \hat\mu_n^{\text{PM}}(s)|}{\hat\sigma(s)}.
\]
Lastly, find the empirical \(1-\alpha\) quantile \(\hat q_{1-\alpha}\) of \(\{D^{(r)}\}_{r=1}^R\), and construct the credible bands 
\[
  \left\{\hat\mu_n^{\text{PM}}(s) \pm \hat q_{1-\alpha}\,\hat\sigma(s): s \in \mathcal S\right\}.
\]
Depending on whether the zero function lies entirely within the band, we have another metric to reject \(H_0\) (at probability level \(1-\alpha\)).

\section{Estimation of the covariance function}\label{sec:covariance-estimation} 

So far we have treated \(K\) as known, but in practice we rarely have access to the complete covariance function. The \acs{FDA} literature offers a variety of estimation techniques depending on the application and available data, such as maximum likelihood, \acf{FPCA}, and smooth interpolation methods \citep[e.g.][]{li2010uniform, xiao2018fast, wong2019nonparametric,mbaka2025estimating}. Parametric approaches---both frequentist and Bayesian---are also widely used, but they often require extra assumptions that lead to more complex models. We advocate for a simpler plug-in strategy in which an estimated covariance is computed from the data, and then used in our model as if it were the true covariance. The performance of the plug-in test for finite sample sizes will depend on how well the estimated covariance approximates \(K\), but as we will see in Section~\ref{sec:consistency-plug-in}, the asymptotic properties of the test are unaffected if the estimator itself is uniformly consistent.

Denote the sample mean by \(\bar X_n(\cdot) = n^{-1}\sum_i X_i(\cdot)\) and consider the standard empirical covariance estimator
\[
\hat C_n(t,s) = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i(t)-\bar X_n(t))(X_i(s) - \bar X_n(s)).
\]
From a computational perspective we only need to consider its discretized counterpart on the grid \(\mathcal S\), namely 
\[
  \hat{\mathbf C}_n=\left[\hat{C}_n(s_g, s_h)\right]_{g,h=1}^G,
\]
which approximates the discretized covariance \(\mathbf K=[K(s_g, s_h)]_{g,h=1}^G\). However, in functional settings the sample size is typically small relative to the number of grid points, so the empirical covariance can be highly rank-deficient. A common regularization technique consists on shrinking towards a well-conditioned target to lift the near-zero eigenvalues, improving the quality and stability of the estimator. The \acf{OAS} estimator \citep{chen2010shrinkage} is a popular choice for this purpose; its closed-form expression on the grid is
\[
\hat{\mathbf K}_n^{\text{OAS}} = (1-\hat\rho_n)\hat{\mathbf C}_n + \hat\rho_n \frac{\tr(\hat{\mathbf C}_n)}{G}I_G,
\]
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho-oas}
\hat\rho_n = \min\left\{1,\, \frac{(1-2/G)\tr(\hat{\mathbf C}_n^2) + \tr^2(\hat{\mathbf C}_n)}{(n+1-2/G)\left[\tr(\hat{\mathbf C}_n^2) - \tr^2(\hat{\mathbf C}_n)/G \right]}\right\}.
\end{equation}

A convenient consequence of this formulation is that the resulting covariance matrix is strictly positive definite (except in pathological cases). Moreover, it can be shown that as the sample size grows the \acs{OAS} estimator increasingly resembles the empirical covariance estimator \(\hat{\mathbf C}_n\), which is the expected behavior since there is less and less need for regularization.

\subsection{Additional methods for estimating the covariance function}

Although covariance estimation techniques fall outside the scope of this thesis, we briefly review some of the most commonly used methods in the literature.

\subsubsection*{Estimation with one trajectory}

There are some particular cases in which \(K\) can be consistently estimated from just one trajectory of the process, as long as it is observed on an arbitrarily fine grid of time instants. In this case, consistency is understood as the grid becomes denser (i.e.\ \(G\to\infty\)), in the so-called high frequency regime (sometimes referred to as ``infill asymptotics''), and results are tailored to specific covariance functions. For example, the Brownian motion scale parameter can be estimated consistently via the quadratic variation of the process \citep{miao2006estimation}. The Hurst and scale parameters in a fractional Brownian motion can also be estimated consistently, both in a noiseless setting \citep{coeurjolly2001estimating, brouste2018local} and with additive or continuous noise, with some vanishing assumptions on the noise level \citep{szymanski2024optimal, chigansky2023estimation, dozzi2015asymptotic}. For the squared exponential kernel, the lengthscale parameter can be consistently estimated under mild conditions, even when the variance is unknown \citep{loh2000estimating}.

Additionally, there are some general results for the Matérn class of kernels, which have the form
\[
K_{\nu,\alpha, \sigma^2}(t,s) = \frac{\sigma^2(\alpha|t-s|)^\nu}{\Gamma(\nu)2^{\nu-1}}K_\nu(\alpha|t-s|), 
\]
where \(\sigma^2>0\) is the variance, \(\alpha>0\) is the scale, \(\nu>0\) is the smoothness, \(\Gamma(\cdot)\) is the Gamma function, and \(K_\nu(\cdot)\) is the modified Bessel function of the second kind of order \(\nu\). Note that the Ornstein--Uhlenbeck kernel is a particular case of a Matérn kernel with \(\nu=1/2\). By definition, two equivalent Gaussian measures cannot be distinguished by a single trajectory, regardless of how dense the observation grid is. Motivated by this result, \textit{microergodic parameters} are defined as those functions of the parameters that, when different, produce orthogonal underlying measures \citep[see][Section~4]{bachoc2021asymptotic}. In the case of Matérn kernels, neither \(\sigma^2\) nor \(\alpha\) is microergodic, and thus these parameters are not consistently estimable from one trajectory. However, \(\sigma^2\alpha^{2\nu}\) is microergodic, and it turns out that it can be estimated consistently via maximum likelihood \citep{zhang2004inconsistent}.

\subsubsection*{Regularized estimation}

A well-known method to regularize the empirical covariance estimator \(\hat C_n\) is the Ledoit--Wolf procedure \citep{ledoit2004well}, which contemplates an estimator of the form
\[
\hat K_n^{\text{LW}}(t,s) = (1-\rho_n)\hat C_n(t,s) + \rho_n \Gamma(t,s),
\]
where \(\rho_n \in [0,1]\) is a coefficient that controls the amount of shrinkage and \(\Gamma\) is a well-conditioned target. From a discretized point of view, the authors derive an optimal shrinkage intensity for a target \(\Gamma =\sigma^2 I\) with \(\sigma^2>0\), obtained as the solution to an optimization problem to asymptotically minimize the mean squared error between the true and estimated covariances (measured in terms of the Frobenius norm). Moreover, they also gave their estimator a Bayesian interpretation: the prior belief that the covariance is a scalar multiple of the identity is weighted by the sample information in the form of the empirical covariance. Building on this work, \citet{chen2010shrinkage} proposed an improved version that achieves a lower mean squared error when the data are Gaussian, which is the \acs{OAS} estimator described earlier.

\subsection*{Bayesian estimation}

Apart from the frequentist approaches outlined above, we could of course follow a Bayesian methodology to estimate the covariance function, for example by considering an inverse Wishart prior on it \citep[e.g.][]{yang2016smoothing}. Alternatively, we could parametrize the covariance, say \(K=K_\phi\) with \(\phi\in\Phi \subseteq \R^D\). Then, we have two choices:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Consider \(\phi\) as a new Bayesian variable in our model with a prior \(\Pi(\phi)\), adapt the likelihood (conditional on \(\phi\)) and equations accordingly, and integrate \(\phi\) into the \acs{RJMCMC} algorithm to produce posterior samples.
  \item Compute a Bayesian estimator \(\hat\phi\) from the data, fix \(K=K_{\hat\phi}\) and proceed as if the covariance function were known.
\end{enumerate}

The first option is the natural one in a Bayesian framework, but it presents important practical problems in our setting. First, since the mean function is a member of the \acs{RKHS} \(\mathcal H(K_\phi)\), it would now be parametrized as
\[
\mu_{p, \tau, b, \phi}(\cdot) = \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j K_\phi(t_j, \cdot),
\]
so that \(\phi\) dictates both the data variance \(K_\phi\) and the shape of the basis functions \(K_\phi(t_j, \cdot)\) in the finite-dimensional expansion of the mean. This could cause a confounding issue in which the \acs{RJMCMC} sampler could not decide a suitable value for \(\phi\) that describes both effects accurately. For example, a small change \(\phi\to\tilde\phi\) could cause the current basis functions \(K_{\tilde\phi}(t_j, \cdot)\) to be a bad fit for the mean, so the move would most likely be rejected, and the sampler could have a high rejection rate and poor mixing. Moreover, we would need to update the values of \(\phi\) (and hence \(K_\phi\)) on each iteration, re-computing all matrices and inverses that depend on it, which would come at a high computational cost.

The second option, while not ideal, can still be interpreted in a pseudo-Bayesian way if we follow an Empirical Bayes approach. The idea is to compute the estimator by maximizing the marginal likelihood with respect to \(\phi\), i.e., \(\hat\phi = \arg\max_\phi m(X_{1:n}|\phi)\), using numerical integration or standard Monte Carlo techniques. Even though we would only have a single point estimate for \(\phi\), we could try to propagate the uncertainty somewhat with a Laplace approximation of the form \(\phi \sim \mathcal N(\hat\phi, \widehat{Var}(\hat\phi))\) (or similar), and average across samples of this distribution when computing the posterior metrics.


\section{Asymptotic theory}\label{sec:consistency-bft}

To assess the frequentist behavior of the proposed Bayesian test, we study posterior consistency and posterior contraction rates of the Bayes factor, which depend only on the large-sample behavior of the posterior probability of \(H_0\) (or equivalently, of \(H_1\)). In this case, a posterior contraction rate quantifies how fast the posterior mass concentrates around the true hypothesis as \(n\to\infty\); we will use the concept of stochastic boundedness (see Definition~\ref{def:stochastic-boundedness}) to evaluate contraction rates. 

As an abuse of notation, we still write \(\pp_0\) and \(\pp_\mu\) for the product measures \(\pp_0^{(n)}\) and \(\pp_{\mu}^{(n)}\) of \((X_1,X_2,\dots, X_n)\), respectively, when the dependence on the sample size \(n\) is clear from context. Moreover, we let \(\pp_0^\infty\) denote the distribution of \((X_1,X_2,\dots)\) under \(H_0\). The proofs of the theoretical results are deferred to Section~\ref{sec:proofs-bft}.

We already established that the sample paths of the process \(X\) are supposed to be continuous, but we state this fact as an explicit assumption to emphasize its importance in the theoretical guarantees.

\begin{assumption}\label{ass:continuous-paths}
  The sample paths \(t \mapsto X(t)\) are continuous almost surely.
\end{assumption}

\subsection{Consistency and contraction rates for known covariance}\label{sec:consistency-known}

We first focus on the case where \(H_0\) is true, that is, \(X_1,\dots,X_n \sim \gp(0, K)\) i.i.d.\ with distribution \(\pp_0\). As it is often the case in Bayesian tests with a point-null hypothesis, posterior consistency holds if \(\Pi(H_0)>0\), through a direct application of Doob's theorem \citep[][Theorem 10.24]{dass2004note,ghosal2017fundamentals}. We provide an explicit proof for completeness.

\begin{theorem}\label{th:consistency-h0}
  \marginproof{proof:consistency-h0}
  Under \(H_0\), we have 
  \[
    \lim_{n\to\infty} \Pi_n(H_1\mid X_{1:n}) = 0\quad \pp_0^\infty-\text{a.s.}
  \]
\end{theorem}

To study posterior contraction rates, we impose additional assumptions on the prior distributions.

\begin{assumption}\label{ass:compact-support-tau}
  The prior measure on the time instants, \(\Pi_\tau(\cdot|p)\), is supported on a compact subset \(\mathcal C_p\subset \mathcal T_p\), that is, \(\Pi_\tau(\mathcal C_p|p)=1\) for all \(p\) such that \(\Pi(p)>0\).
\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}\label{ass:prior-p-finite}
  The prior \(\Pi(p)\) has finite support, i.e., \(\Pi(p) = 0\) for all \(p > P_{\max}\).
\end{assumption}

Note that~\ref{ass:compact-support-tau} is verified, for example, by enforcing a uniform minimum separation on the time points in \(\tau\) (which already happens in practice on the finite grid \(\mathcal S\)). Indeed, for a fixed \(\delta>0\) one can consider the compact set
\[
\mathcal C_p(\delta) = \{\tau\in [0,1]^p: t_1 \geq \delta,\ t_p \leq 1 - \delta,\ t_{j+1} - t_j \geq \delta\ \text{for}\ j=1,\dots,p-1\}.
\]
On the other hand,~\ref{ass:prior-p-finite} is effectively a truncation on the model dimension, but it is not as restrictive as it may seem. Firstly, it is a standard assumption in the literature, and secondly, our model is designed for simplicity and parsimony, so we in fact encourage fewer components. In any event, the unknown value of \(p\) can still vary over a set as large as needed (e.g.\ we can take \(P_{\max}=|\mathcal S|\)). With these assumptions in mind, we can show that under \(H_0\) the posterior probability of the alternative hypothesis decays polynomially in the sample size. Let \(p_{\min}=\min\{p\in \N: \Pi(p)>0\}\).

\begin{theorem}\label{th:consistency-rates-h0}
  \marginproof{proof:consistency-rates-h0}
  Suppose~\ref{ass:continuous-paths}--\ref{ass:prior-p-finite} hold. Then, under \(H_0\) we have 
  \[
    \Pi_n(H_1\mid X_{1:n}) = O_{\pp_0}(n^{-p_{\min}/2}).
  \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
  If \(p_{\min}=1\) we recover the standard parametric rate \(n^{-1/2}\). Even though the asymptotic rate \(O_{\pp_0}(n^{-p_{\min}/2})\) would suggest that it is better to ignore low values of \(p\) in the prior to improve consistency speed, finite-sample performance can be hurt by considering many components, since they introduce increasingly high collinearity. Moreover, removing low-dimensional alternatives makes the test more conservative and reduces interpretability.
\end{remark}

We now turn to the case where \(H_1\) is true. Suppose that the model is well-specified and denote the true mean function by \(\mu_*\in \Hcal(K) \setminus \{0\}\), so that \(X_1,\dots,X_n \sim \gp(\mu_*, K)\) i.i.d.\ with distribution \(\pp_{\mu_*}\) (note that \(\mu_*\in\Hcal(K)\setminus \Hcal_0(K)\) is allowed). In this situation we need to make sure that the prior places positive mass on the true mean, so the support cannot be restricted.

\begin{assumption}\label{ass:positive-prior-mass}
  The prior distribution \(\Pi\) charges open sets, i.e., \(\Pi(p)>0\) for all \(p\in\N\) and \(\Pi_{\tau,b}^{\text{slab}}(U|p)>0\) for all open sets \(U \subseteq \mathcal T_p\times \R^p\), where \(\Pi_{\tau,b}^{\text{slab}}(\cdot|p)\) is the slab part of the joint prior measure on \((\tau, b)\).
  
\end{assumption}

This mild condition is already sufficient to derive exponential contraction rates (which immediately guarantees posterior consistency). The proof relies on the fact that \(\Hcal_0(K)\) is dense in \(\Hcal(K)\), so we can always find an approximation \(\mu_{\theta}\in\Hcal_0(K)\) as close as desired to \(\mu_*\) in the \acs{RKHS} norm.

\begin{theorem}\label{th:consistency-rates-h1}
  \marginproof{proof:consistency-rates-h1}
  Suppose~\ref{ass:continuous-paths} and~\ref{ass:positive-prior-mass} hold. Then, under \(H_1\) we have
  \[
    \Pi_n(H_0\mid X_{1:n}) = O_{\pp_{\mu_*}}(e^{-n\gamma}),
  \]
  for any \(0 < \gamma < \frac{1}{2}\|\mu_*\|^2_K\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
  The upper bound on \(\gamma\) is not arbitrary; we will show in Section~\ref{sec:optimality} that it is in fact an information-theoretic limit. We also note that, as expected, the rate will be faster when it is easier to distinguish \(\mu_*\) from the zero function, that is, the larger \(\|\mu_*\|_K\) is.
\end{remark}

It is worth mentioning that~\ref{ass:positive-prior-mass} is a bit more restrictive than needed, and it cannot hold simultaneously with the assumptions of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-rates-h0}. Looking carefully at the proof of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-rates-h1}, one notices that the approximated mean function \(\mu_\theta\) is only required to satisfy the condition \(\|\mu_* - \mu_{\theta}\|^2_K < \|\mu_*\|^2_K\). To make the result compatible with the practical assumptions~\ref{ass:compact-support-tau} and~\ref{ass:prior-p-finite}, we could replace~\ref{ass:positive-prior-mass} by the following condition.

\begin{assumptionalt}\label{ass:positive-prior-mass-alt}
  There exists \(\theta_0=(p_0,\tau_0,b_0)\) such that \(p_0\leq P_{\max}\), \(\tau_0\in \mathcal C_{p_0}\), \(b_0\in\R^{p_0} \setminus \{0\}\) and \(\|\mu_* - \mu_{\theta_0}\|^2_K < \|\mu_*\|^2_K\). Moreover, for each \(p\leq P_{\max}\) we have \(\Pi(p)>0\) and \(\Pi_{\tau,b}^{\text{slab}}(\cdot|p)\) has full support on \(\mathcal C_p \times \R^p\).
\end{assumptionalt}

The full support condition is trivially satisfied with a standard choice of priors. As for the approximation constraint, we claim that in practice it reduces to a very minor requirement on the grid \(\mathcal S\). Fix \(p_0=1\), consider any single time point \(s\) with \(\tau_0=(s)\in \mathcal C_1\), and set \(b_0=(\mu_*(s)/K(s,s))\), which is well-defined because \(K\) is strictly positive definite. Then, by expanding the \acs{RKHS} norm and using the reproducing property, we can write
\[
\|\mu_* - \mu_{\theta_0}\|^2_K = \|\mu_*\|^2_K + \|\mu_{\theta_0}\|^2_K - 2\dotprod{\mu_*}{\mu_{\theta_0}}_K = \|\mu_*\|^2_K - \frac{\mu_*(s)^2}{K(s,s)},
\]
which is smaller than \(\|\mu_*\|^2_K\) as long as \(\mu_*(s)\neq 0\). Thus,~\ref{ass:positive-prior-mass-alt} holds whenever there exists at least one point in \(\mathcal C_1\) (which is effectively equal to \(\mathcal S\)) where \(\mu_*\) does not vanish. 

We can state a variant of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-rates-h1} under this alternative assumption, in which the exponential rate now includes an additional approximation slack.

\begin{theorem}\label{th:consistency-rates-h1-alt}
  \marginproof{proof:consistency-rates-h1-alt}
  Suppose~\ref{ass:continuous-paths}--\ref{ass:prior-p-finite} and~\ref{ass:positive-prior-mass-alt} hold. Then, under \(H_1\) we have
  \[
    \Pi_n(H_0\mid X_{1:n}) = O_{\pp_{\mu_*}}(e^{-n\gamma}),
  \]
  for any \(0 < \gamma < \frac{1}{2}(\|\mu_*\|^2_K - \|\mu_* - \mu_{\theta_0}\|^2_K)\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
  By considering~\ref{ass:positive-prior-mass-alt} instead of~\ref{ass:positive-prior-mass} we gain a simpler condition that does not conflict with the assumptions of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-rates-h0}, but the price we pay is a slower contraction rate under \(H_1\) (unless \(\mu_* \in \Hcal_0(K)\)). Moreover, even though~\ref{ass:positive-prior-mass-alt} can be satisfied with just \(p_0=1\), the rate improves with the quality of the approximation (i.e.\ when \(\|\mu_* - \mu_{\theta_0}\|^2_K\) is small), so we can expect much faster rates if the condition holds for bigger values of \(p_0\).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Consistency and contraction rates for plug-in covariance}\label{sec:consistency-plug-in}

We extend the results of Section~\ref{sec:consistency-known} to the case in which the true covariance function \(K(t,s)\) is unknown and replaced by a symmetric plug-in estimator \(\hat K_n(t,s)\), which need not be strictly positive definite. For any \(\tau\in\mathcal T_p\), let \(\hat K_{n,\tau} = [\hat K_n(t_j, t_k)]_{j,k=1}^p\) be the plug-in analog of the matrix \(K_\tau\) defined in~\eqref{eq:likelihood}. We will see that as long as we have uniform consistency of \(\hat K_{n,\tau}\) to \(K_\tau\), the approximation effect is negligible and the posterior contraction rates stay the same. Therefore, we impose the following condition.

\begin{assumption}\label{ass:uniform-consistency-plug-in}
  For every \(p\) with \(\Pi(p)>0\) and any compact set \(\mathcal C_p\subset \mathcal T_p\), it holds that
  \[
  \sup_{\tau\in \mathcal C_p}\|\hat K_{n,\tau} - K_\tau\| \stackrel{\pp}{\longrightarrow} 0.
  \]
\end{assumption}

It can be shown that both the empirical covariance estimator \(\hat C_n\) and the \acs{OAS} estimator described in Section~\ref{sec:covariance-estimation} satisfy the stronger condition of almost sure uniform convergence. Consider for each \(\tau\in\mathcal T_p\) the discretized matrices
\[
\hat{C}_{n,\tau} = [\hat C_n(t_j, t_k)]_{j,k=1}^p \quad \text{and}\quad \hat{K}_{n,\tau}^{\text{OAS}} = (1-\hat\rho_{n,\tau})\hat C_{n,\tau} + \hat\rho_{n,\tau}\frac{\tr(\hat C_{n,\tau})}{p}I_p,
\]
where \(\hat\rho_{n,\tau}\in[0,1]\) is the \acs{OAS} shrinkage coefficient computed via the analogue of~\eqref{eq:rho-oas} with \(\hat{\mathbf C}_n\) replaced by \(\hat{C}_{n,\tau}\) and \(G\) replaced by \(p\).

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:sample-cov-matrix-convergence}
  \marginproof{proof:sample-cov-matrix-convergence}
  For any matrix norm \(\|\cdot\|\) and any compact set \(\mathcal C_p\subset \mathcal T_p\), we have:
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item \(\|\hat {\mathbf C}_n - \mathbf K\| \to 0\) a.s.\ and \(\sup_{\tau\in \mathcal C_p} \|\hat C_{n,\tau} - K_\tau\| \to 0\) a.s.
    \item \(\|\hat{\mathbf K}^{\emph{OAS}}_n - \mathbf K\| \to 0\) a.s.\ and \(\sup_{\tau\in \mathcal C_p} \|\hat K^{\emph{OAS}}_{n,\tau} - K_\tau\| \to 0\) a.s.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

This convergence holds under any \(\gp(\mu, K)\) distribution with continuous sample paths, so both estimators satisfy~\ref{ass:uniform-consistency-plug-in} simultaneously under \(\pp_0\) and \(\pp_{\mu_*}\). The following results simply state that the contraction rates for the plug-in posterior \(\hat \Pi_n(\cdot|X_{1:n})\) computed using a suitable \(\hat K_n\) do not degrade. The proofs are a refinement of the corresponding ones for the known covariance case, in which the plug-in error is identified and controlled. 

\begin{theorem}\label{th:consistency-rates-h0-plugin}
  \marginproof{proof:consistency-rates-h0-plugin}
  Suppose~\ref{ass:continuous-paths}--\ref{ass:prior-p-finite} and~\ref{ass:uniform-consistency-plug-in} hold. Then, under \(H_0\) we have
  \[
    \hat \Pi_n(H_1\mid X_{1:n}) = O_{\pp_0}(n^{-p_{\min}/2}).
  \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}\label{th:consistency-rates-h1-plugin-1}
  \marginproof{proof:consistency-rates-h1-plugin-1}
  Suppose~\ref{ass:continuous-paths},~\ref{ass:positive-prior-mass} and~\ref{ass:uniform-consistency-plug-in} hold. Then, under \(H_1\) we have
  \[
    \hat \Pi_n(H_0\mid X_{1:n}) = O_{\pp_{\mu_*}}(e^{-n\gamma}),
  \]
  for any \(0<\gamma<\frac{1}{2}\|\mu_*\|_K^2\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}\label{th:consistency-rates-h1-plugin-2}
  \marginproof{proof:consistency-rates-h1-plugin-2}
  Suppose~\ref{ass:continuous-paths}--\ref{ass:prior-p-finite},~\ref{ass:positive-prior-mass-alt} and~\ref{ass:uniform-consistency-plug-in} hold. Then, under \(H_1\) we have
  \[
    \hat \Pi_n(H_0\mid X_{1:n}) = O_{\pp_{\mu_*}}(e^{-n\gamma}),
  \]
  for any \(0<\gamma<\frac{1}{2}(\|\mu_*\|_K^2 - \|\mu_* - \mu_{\theta_0}\|_K^2)\).
\end{theorem}

\subsection{On optimality of the contraction rates}\label{sec:optimality}

An aspect of the theoretical exposition that might stand out is the asymmetry of the contraction rates: polynomial under \(H_0\) and exponential under \(H_1\). Intuitively, this disparity can be traced to the spike-and-slab prior~\eqref{eq:prior-b} placed on the coefficient vector \(b\). Because the slab prior assigns nonnegligible mass arbitrarily close to the null region \(\{b=0\}\), under \(H_0\) the model struggles to rule out small nonzero mean functions, thereby slowing posterior concentration to the null. In contrast, under \(H_1\) the spike prior---a point mass at zero---is well separated from the truth, so the evidence accumulates much faster in favor of the alternative. This heuristic argument is made precise for example by \citet{johnson2010use}, who refer to such priors as ``local alternative priors''. Under some regularity conditions, they show that in this case the best achievable rate under a point-null is polynomial, whereas under the alternative the rate is typically exponential.

A natural follow-up question is whether the exponential rate under \(H_1\) can be improved, and the answer is negative. For a sequence of frequentist tests in the Neyman--Pearson sense (with bounded type I error probability) comparing a point-null against a simple alternative, the Chernoff--Stein lemma \citep[Theorem 11.8.3]{cover2006elements} states that the optimal decay rate of the type II error probability is exponential, with exponent constant equal to the Kullback--Leibler divergence between the underlying probability distributions. We show in the result below that this information-theoretic bound also applies to our Bayesian test, by thresholding the posterior to produce a frequentist test under the lemma's purview. Moreover, the Kullback--Leibler divergence in question is precisely \(\dkl(\pp_{\mu_*} \,\|\,\pp_0)=\frac{1}{2}\|\mu_*\|_K^2\), so the rate established in Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-rates-h1} is arbitrarily close to the theoretical optimum.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:optimality-rates-h1}
  \marginproof{proof:optimality-rates-h1}
  Under \(H_1\), there is no \(\zeta > \frac{1}{2}\|\mu_*\|_K^2\) such that 
  \[
    \Pi_n(H_0\mid X_{1:n}) = O_{\pp_{\mu_*}}(e^{-n\zeta}).
  \] 
\end{proposition}

\section{Experimental results}\label{sec:experiments-bft}

We illustrate the finite-sample performance of the proposed Bayesian test through a series of experiments with simulated and real data.\footnote{The Python code is available at \url{https://github.com/antcc/rk-bt-jump}.} In both situations the covariance function is assumed unknown and estimated using the plug-in \acs{OAS} estimator. The \acs{RJMCMC} sampling procedure is again implemented in Python with the help of the \slsc{Eryn} library \citep{karnesis2023eryn}. We deployed 64 walkers and ran each one for 2500 steps, discarding the first 1500 samples as burn-in (though convergence was typically achieved in fewer steps). Additional hyperparameters for the sampler were selected based on recommendations from the authors and a small set of trial runs (see Chapter~\ref{ch:rjmcmc} for details).

\subsection{Simulation study} The simulated data comes from three different \acs{GPs}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A Brownian motion (\slsc{BM}) with variance \(\sigma^2=1.5\).
  \item An Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process (\slsc{OU}) with variance \(\sigma^2=1.5\) and scale \(\ell=0.2\).
  \item A \acs{GP} with a squared exponential kernel (\slsc{SE}), variance \(\sigma^2=1.5\) and scale \(\ell=0.2\).
\end{enumerate}
We draw \(n=50\) functional samples from each distribution, observed on a discretized grid of \(G=100\) equispaced points on \([0,1]\). The mean function under \(H_0\) is \(\mu_0\equiv 0\), and under \(H_1\) we consider two alternatives: a function in \(\Hcal_0(K)\) given by
\[
  \mu_*^{\text{RKHS}}(t) = 0.2K(0.15, t) - 0.3K(0.4, t) + 0.5K(0.75, t)
\]
where \(K\) is the corresponding covariance kernel in each case, and a periodic function
\[
  \mu_*^{\text{P}}(t) = 0.3\sin(2\pi t),
\]

We implement a reduced model with \(P_{\max}=5\) components, a uniform prior distribution \(p\sim\mathcal U\{1,2,3,4,5\}\), and a uniform prior for the time instants as well (restricted to the grid points in \(\mathcal S\)). Moreover, we assume for simplicity that the null and alternative hypotheses are a priori equally likely, so that \(\pi_0=1-\pi_0=1/2\). When choosing the crucial hyperparameter \(\eta\) one has to be wary of Lindley's paradox \citep{lindley1957statistical}. We propose to compute it from the discretized data through the formula
\[
\hat \eta_n=\eta_0 \sqrt{\frac{\tr(\hat{\mathbf K}_n^{\text{OAS}})}{G}}, \quad \eta_0>0,
\]
which measures the marginal standard deviation of the process, multiplied by a constant scale term. In this way we allow the prior on \(b\) to adapt to the scale of the data; we fix a value of \(\eta_0=1/4\) to allow detection of small signals (preliminary experiments suggest that bigger signals are easily recognized regardless of the value of \(\eta_0\)). Since \(\hat\eta_n\) converges in probability to a finite positive constant, this data-dependent choice does not affect the asymptotic properties of the test. Indeed, since \(\tr(\hat{\mathbf K}_n^{\text{OAS}})=\tr({\hat{\mathbf C}_n})\) and \(\tr(\hat{\mathbf C}_n) \to \tr({\mathbf K})\) by Proposition~\ref{prop:sample-cov-matrix-convergence}, we have \(\hat \eta_n \to \eta_\infty > 0\). We can refine the proofs in Section~\ref{sec:proofs-bft} by restricting the arguments to the high-probability event \(\{\eta_\infty/2 \leq \hat\eta_n \leq 2\eta_\infty\}\), on which \(\hat\eta_n\) is bounded away from zero and infinity. Since all quantities appearing in the proofs that depend on \(\eta\) do so in a continuous manner, the constants involved in the corresponding bounds can be chosen uniformly over the compact interval \([\eta_\infty/2, 2\eta_\infty]\), and the consistency arguments carry over without further modification.

Then, we compare our method (which we call \slsc{RKBT}) to other one-sample testing procedures in the literature:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Pointwise \(t\)-test over all grid points with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (\slsc{Bon-t}). This is the most naive approach, used only as a baseline.
  \item Global test based on the squared \(L^2\)-norm \(\|\Delta_n\|_2^2 = n\int_0^1 \bar{X}_n^2(t)\, dt\), whose null distribution is a \(\chi^2\)-type mixture (\slsc{L2}).
  \item A version of the \(L^2\) test with projections through \acs{FPCA} (\slsc{L2-FPCA}).
  \item Global test based on the regularized statistic \(F_n=\|\Delta_n\|_2^2/\tr(\hat{K}_n)\), which follows an \(F\)-type mixture distribution under \(H_0\) (\slsc{F}).
  \item Standard Bayesian spike-and-slab test with dimension reduction via \acs{FPCA} and a conjugate \(g\)-prior under \(H_1\) (\slsc{Bayes-FPCA}) 
\end{itemize}
The frequentist competitors are primarily those outlined by \citet{zhang2013analysis}, whereas the Bayesian competitor arguably represents a novel---albeit simple---approach. A detailed breakdown of the comparison methods is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:comparison-test}.

Performance is measured through several frequentist metrics, repeating the whole procedure 100 times with different random seeds. We compare the rejection rate under \(H_0\) (type I error) and under \(H_1\) (power), as well as the \acf{AUC} metric \citep{krzanowski2009roc}. The frequentist methods are calibrated to the nominal size of \(\alpha=0.05\), and the Bayesian methods are thresholded through the estimated Bayes factor to produce a binary accept/reject decision. Specifically, we use the standard rejection region provided by Jeffreys' scale (see Table~\ref{tab:jeffreys-scale}), which is given by \(\{\hat{{\bff}}_{10}>3\}\) and corresponds to \(\{\hat\pi_{\text{null}}<1/4\}\) with our prior assumptions. 

The results of the simulation study are presented in Table~\ref{tab:metrics-rkhs} and Table~\ref{tab:metrics-p}. We can see that our \slsc{RKBT} method has great control of the type I error, while also achieving decent power and \acs{AUC} in most cases, despite having only a relatively low number of components to work with. Moreover, the comparison methods that obtain a higher power generally do so at the expense of a greater type I error, which may be undesirable. Note that we could increase the power of our test by lowering the rejection threshold, which is fairly conservative, to a point where we still maintain an acceptable type I error (i.e.\ below 5\%).

\subsection{Real data application}

The metrics in the simulation study are frequentist in nature, but the strength of our Bayesian test lies in the interpretability and the ability to quantify uncertainty. To analyze this aspect, we present a brief case study with real climate data. We then form pairwise differences between the decade-specific curves and apply our test to assess the null hypothesis of no difference in the mean temperature. We use the same prior distributions as in the simulations, but we allow \(P_{\max}=12\) and set \(\pi_0=1/3\), to convey the widespread belief that the temperature is actually increasing globally.

We can also see, for example, that the model gives very low posterior probability to differences of less than \(0.1^\circ\mathrm{C}\) from the 2010s to the 1990s, signalling that the discrepancy is bigger in that case. Note that around five components (months) seem to be sufficient to model the temperature differences, supporting our parsimony assumptions.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:conclusions}

In this chapter we have described a Bayesian test for the mean \(\mu\) of a Gaussian process, observed through i.i.d.\ functional samples. The proposed model hinges on a finite-dimensional representation enabled by the reasonable assumption that \(\mu\in\Hcal(K)\), where \(\Hcal(K)\) is the \acs{RKHS} associated with the covariance function \(K\) of the process. The case \(\mu\notin \Hcal(K)\), though not treated here, is also interesting in its own right. When the true mean function \(\mu_*\) lies outside \(\Hcal(K)\), by the Feldman--Hájek dichotomy the measures \(\pp_{\mu_*}\) and \(\pp_0\) are mutually singular, so there exists a Borel set \(S\) such that \(\pp_{\mu_*}(S)=1\) and \(\pp_0(S)=0\). In principle, we could define the test \(\phi(X)=\mathds{1}\{X\in S\}\), which has both type I and type II errors equal to zero, and can separate \(\pp_{\mu_*}\) and \(\pp_0\) perfectly with a single full observation of the process. This is an example of a ``near-perfect'' testing situation, in which the functional nature of the data allows exact verification of the null hypothesis in theory. In a discretized setting this effect might not be detectable with a finite grid, but it can manifest as the grid becomes dense \citep{torrecilla2020optimal}. Other instances of this phenomenon applied to functional data classification are studied, for example, by \citet{delaigle2012achieving} or \citet{berrendero2018use}. 

The Bayesian formulation of the test is flexible enough to accommodate a range of priors, fixing only the so-called ``local'' spike-and-slab prior on the coefficient vector \(b\), which is a common choice in multivariate settings. Other authors \citep[e.g.][]{johnson2010use} have objected to local alternative priors on a philosophical level, proposing different nonlocal alternatives. However, adopting them in our context would render the closed-form marginalization of \(b\) invalid, which is central to our computational strategy. 

Our approach connects the functional testing problem to a variable-dimension model in a principled way, and is theoretically backed by near-optimal posterior consistency results. These findings confirm that the Bayes factor-based test is asymptotically reliable under both the null and the alternative, and they provide a more refined description of how quickly evidence accumulates in favor of the correct hypothesis. This analysis is complemented by numerical studies with a tractable computational implementation, showing that our test is on a par with other methods in the literature. Lastly, we have also illustrated how the Bayesian aspect of the model can be used to quantify the uncertainty in the posterior distribution and interpret the results of the test, since we have access to a continuous posterior probability that measures the strength of evidence against the null.

\section{Proofs}\label{sec:proofs-bft}

Here we present the proofs of the theoretical results in this chapter. Bear in mind that the covariance function \(K\) is supposed to be strictly positive definite and continuous. Continuity is inherited by all functions in the \acs{RKHS} \citep[Corollary~5]{berlinet2004reproducing}, so \(\mu\in\Hcal(K)\) is also continuous. In fact, continuity of \(K\) and \(\mu\) always holds for Gaussian data with continuous sample paths.\footnote{Gaussianity is not essential for the continuity of \(K\) and \(\mu\). Under~\ref{ass:continuous-paths}, finiteness of \(\E[\sup_t X(t)^2]\) suffices, which indeed holds automatically for Gaussian data \citep{landau1970supremum}.} To see this, let \(t_n\to t\) and \(u_n \to u\) in \([0,1]\). Under~\ref{ass:continuous-paths},
\[
  (X(t_n), X(u_n))\to (X(t), X(u))\quad \text{in distribution},
\]
and by the Portmanteau lemma, the corresponding characteristic functions converge pointwise. Hence, \(\mu(t_n)\to \mu(t)\) and \(K(t_n, u_n) \to K(t,u)\), because a Gaussian process is completely determined by its mean and covariance functions.

We now introduce some useful notation. Using Bayes' rule~\eqref{eq:posterior-p-tau} for the posterior of \((p,\tau)\) and writing the normalization constant explicitly, the posterior probability of the alternative is given by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:posterior-slab}
\Pi_n(H_1|X_{1:n})= \frac{(1-\pi_0)m_1(X_{1:n})}{\pi_0 + (1-\pi_0)m_1(X_{1:n})},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:posterior-slab-m1}
m_1(X_{1:n}) = \sum_{p\geq 1} \Pi(p)\int_{\mathcal T_p} \mslab(X_{1:n}\mid p,\tau)\Pi(\tau\mid p) \, d\tau.
\end{equation}


\subsection{Consistency and contraction rates for known covariance}

We start with a proof of consistency under \(H_0\), even though the result is already well-known. As we said before, it is a direct application of Doob's theorem (Theorem~\ref{th:doob}).

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-h0}]\label{proof:consistency-h0}

In this case we do not need continuous trajectories of \(X\), so we can work with \(\mathcal X = L^2[0,1]\). Since the parameter \(\theta\) is not identifiable, we will push the model forward (via the mapping \(\theta \mapsto \mu_\theta\)) and consider the parameter \(\mu\in\Hcal(K)\) and the induced prior \(\Pi^\mu\). Note that the new parameter space \(\Hcal(K)\) is separable because \(K\) is continuous \citep[Corollary~4]{berlinet2004reproducing}, and hence \(\Hcal(K)\) is a Polish space. Moreover, since the data are Gaussian, the mapping \(\mu\mapsto \pp_\mu\) is one-to-one. Now consider a measurable set \(A\subseteq\mathcal X\), and observe that \(\pp_\mu\) is the distribution of \(W + \mu\) with \(W\sim \pp_0\). Furthermore, the embedding \(\Hcal(K) \hookrightarrow L^2[0,1]\) is continuous (again by continuity of \(K\)), so \((x,\mu) \mapsto \mathds{1}_A(x+\mu)\) is jointly measurable. Hence, by Fubini/Tonelli, the function
\[
\mu \mapsto \pp_\mu(A) = \pp_0(W+\mu \in A) = \int_{\mathcal X} \mathds{1}_A(x+\mu) d\pp_0(x)
\]
is measurable.

Then we apply the second part of Doob's theorem with the function \(g(\mu)=\mathds{1}\{\mu\equiv 0\}\), obtaining a set \(\Hcal_*(K)\subseteq \Hcal(K)\) such that \(\Pi^\mu(\Hcal_*(K))=1\) and for all \(\mu_0\in \Hcal_*(K)\), if \(X_1,X_2,\ldots \sim \pp_{\mu_0}\) i.i.d., we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:doob-consistency-theorem-h0}
  \E^\mu[\mathds{1}\{\mu\equiv 0\}\mid X_{1:n}] \to \mathds{1}\{\mu_0\equiv 0\}\quad \pp_{\mu_0}^\infty-\text{a.s.}
\end{equation}
Observe that \(0\in \Hcal_*(K)\), since \(\Pi^\mu(\mu\equiv 0)=\pi_0>0\). Then, by~\eqref{eq:doob-consistency-theorem-h0} applied with \(\mu_0\equiv 0\) we conclude that, if \(X_1, X_2,\ldots \sim \pp_0\) i.i.d., then
\begin{align*}
  \Pi_n(H_0\mid X_{1:n}) &= \Pi_n^\mu(\mu\equiv 0\mid X_{1:n})\\
  &= \E^\mu[\mathds{1}\{\mu\equiv 0\}\mid X_{1:n}] \to 1\quad \pp_0^\infty-\text{a.s.}
\end{align*}
\end{proof}

We now turn to the contraction rates, both under \(H_0\) and \(H_1\). For notational convenience, define
\[
I_{n,p}(U) = \int_U \mslab(X_{1:n}\mid p, \tau)\Pi(\tau\mid p)\, d\tau, \quad \text{for every } U\subseteq \mathcal T_p.
\]

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-rates-h0}]\label{proof:consistency-rates-h0}
Under~\ref{ass:compact-support-tau} and~\ref{ass:prior-p-finite}, the sum \(m_1(X_{1:n})\) simplifies to
\[
m_1(X_{1:n}) = \sum_{p=p_{\min}}^{P_{\max}} \Pi(p) I_{n,p}(\mathcal C_p).
\]

Fix \(p_{\min} \leq p\leq P_{\max}\) and let \(\epsilon>0\). Note that the minimum eigenvalue function \(\tau\mapsto\lambda_{\min}(K_\tau)\) is continuous by Weyl's inequality \citep[Theorem~VI.2.1]{bhatia1996matrix} and the continuity of \(K\). Because \(\mathcal C_p\) is compact and \(K_\tau\) is strictly positive definite, there exists \(\lambda_p>0\) with
\[
\lambda_{\min}(K_\tau)\ge \lambda_p>0\quad\text{for all }\tau\in \mathcal C_p.
\]
Since \(\Lambda_\tau = nK_\tau + \eta^{-2}I_p\), this implies that 
\[
  \lambda_{\min}(\Lambda_\tau) = n\lambda_{\min}(K_\tau) + \eta^{-2} \geq n\lambda_p>0\quad \text{for all } \tau \in \mathcal C_p.
\]
Therefore, the following holds for all \(\tau\in \mathcal C_p\):
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item \(|\Lambda_\tau| \geq (n\lambda_p)^p\).
  \item \(v'\Lambda_{\tau}^{-1}v \leq \frac{1}{n\lambda_p}\|v\|^2\) for any \(v\neq 0\).
\end{enumerate}
Only the second assertion deserves a comment: the Rayleigh quotient of a symmetric matrix is bounded above by its maximum eigenvalue \citep[Theorem 4.2.2]{horn1985matrix}, and \(\lambda_{\max}(\Lambda_\tau^{-1})=(\lambda_{\min}(\Lambda_\tau))^{-1}\). Combining (i), (ii) and~\eqref{eq:m-slab} yields the upper bound
\begin{equation}\label{eq:upper-bound-m-slab}
\mslab(X_{1:n}\mid p,\tau) \leq \eta^{-p}(n\lambda_p)^{-p/2} \exp\left\{\frac{1}{2n\lambda_p}\|S_n(\tau)\|^2\right\}, \ \text{for all } \tau \in \mathcal C_p.
\end{equation}

Now define \(Z_\tau = S_n(\tau)/\sqrt{n}\) for each \(\tau\), and note that \(Z_\tau \sim \mathcal N_p(0, K_\tau)\) under \(H_0\), so its distribution is independent of \(n\). Since
\[
\sup_{\tau \in \mathcal C_p} \frac{\|S_n(\tau)\|^2}{n} = \sup_{\tau \in \mathcal C_p} \|Z_\tau\|^2,
\]
we consider for any \(M>0\) the \(\pp_0\)-measurable event
\begin{equation}\label{eq:event-E-n-M}
E_{M} = \left\{\sup_{\tau\in \mathcal C_p} \|Z_\tau\|^2\leq M\right\}.
\end{equation}
The map \(\tau \mapsto Z_\tau(\omega)\) is continuous for almost every \(\omega\) by~\ref{ass:continuous-paths}, so on the compact set \(\mathcal C_p\) and the points of continuity in \(\omega\), the continuous function \(\tau \mapsto \|Z_\tau(\omega)\|^2\) attains its supremum. Then, \(\sup_{\tau \in \mathcal C_p} \|Z_\tau\|^2 < \infty\) almost surely, which trivially implies that we can choose \(M\) large enough so that the complement \(E_{M}^c\) is negligible in probability, say \(\pp_0(E_{M}^c) < \epsilon\).

On the other hand, on \(E_{M}\) the upper bound in~\eqref{eq:upper-bound-m-slab} reduces to the deterministic bound
\begin{equation}\label{eq:deterministic-bound-sup-mslab}
\sup_{\tau \in \mathcal C_p} \mslab(X_{1:n}\mid p,\tau) \leq \eta^{-p}(n\lambda_p)^{-p/2}\exp\left\{\frac{M}{2\lambda_p}\right\}.
\end{equation}
Since \(\Pi_\tau(\mathcal C_p|p)= 1\), by~\eqref{eq:deterministic-bound-sup-mslab} we have (still on the event \(E_{M}\))
\[
I_{n,p}(\mathcal C_p)\leq \sup_{\tau\in\mathcal C_p} \mslab(X_{1:n}\mid p,\tau) \int_{\mathcal C_p} \Pi(\tau\mid p)\, d\tau \leq Dn^{-p/2},
\]
where \(D>0\) is a constant independent of \(n\). Hence, for sufficiently large \(n\) we have
\[
\pp_0(I_{n,p}(\mathcal C_p) > Dn^{-p/2}) \leq \pp_0(E_{M}^c ) < \epsilon,
\]
so \(I_{n,p}(\mathcal C_p)=O_{\pp_0}(n^{-p/2})\). Write \(I_{n,p}(\mathcal C_p)=n^{-p/2}Y_{n,p}\), where by definition \(Y_{n,p}=O_{\pp_0}(1)\). Then, because the dominant term in the finite sum \(m_1(X_{1:n})\) is the first term, we have
\[
m_1(X_{1:n}) = \sum_{p=p_{\min}}^{P_{\max}} \Pi(p) n^{-p/2}Y_{n,p} = O_{\pp_0}(n^{-p_{\min}/2}).
\]
Lastly, note that
\[
\Pi_n(H_1\mid X_{1:n}) \leq \frac{1-\pi_0}{\pi_0}m_1(X_{1:n}),
\]
so by the continuous mapping theorem the stochastic bound translates immediately and \(\Pi_n(H_1|X_{1:n})=O_{\pp_0}(n^{-p_{\min}/2})\), as claimed.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-rates-h1}]\label{proof:consistency-rates-h1}
The \acs{RKHS} framework will allow us to work in a finite-dimensional setting, by means of a suitable approximation. First, since \(\Hcal_0(K)\) is dense in \(\Hcal(K)\), for any \(\epsilon>0\) there exists a parameter \(\theta_\epsilon = (p_\epsilon, \tau_\epsilon, b_\epsilon)\) such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:approximation-property-mu}
\|\mu_* - \mu_{\tau_\epsilon, b_\epsilon}\|_K < \epsilon,
\end{equation}
where \(\mu_{\tau_\epsilon, b_\epsilon}\) is shorthand for \(\mu_{\theta_\epsilon}\). Since \(\mu_* \neq 0\), choosing \(\epsilon\) small enough (e.g.\ \(\epsilon\leq\|\mu_*\|_K\)) guarantees that \(\mu_{\tau_\epsilon, b_\epsilon}\neq 0\), and hence \(b_\epsilon \neq 0\). By~\ref{ass:positive-prior-mass}, \(\Pi(p_\epsilon)>0\) and there exists a neighborhood \(U =U_1 \times U_2\) of \((\tau_\epsilon, b_\epsilon)\) within \(\mathcal T_{p_\epsilon}\times \R^{p_\epsilon}\) with positive prior probability. We can (and do) take \(U_1\) and \(U_2\) compact, and arrange \(0\notin U_2\). 

Let \((\tau, b)\in U\), denote \(\mu_*(\tau) = (\mu_*(t_1), \dots, \mu_*(t_{p_\epsilon}))'\) and define \(Z_\tau = (S_n(\tau) - n\mu_*(\tau))/\sqrt{n}\). Note that \(Z_\tau\sim \mathcal N_{p_\epsilon}(0, K_\tau)\) under \(H_1\), so its distribution is independent of \(n\). Substituting in~\eqref{eq:likelihood} yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:log-likelihood-H1}
\log L(X_{1:n}\mid p_\epsilon, \tau, b) = n\mu_*(\tau)'b - \frac{n}{2}b'K_\tau b + \sqrt{n}Z_\tau'b.
\end{equation}
On the one hand, for \((\tau, b)\in U\) define the function
\[
f(\tau, b) = \mu_*(\tau)'b - \frac{1}{2}b'K_\tau b = \mu_*(\tau)'b - \frac{1}{2}\|\mu_{\tau,b}\|^2_K,
\]
and observe that by the reproducing property and the linearity of the inner product we have
\[
\dotprod{\mu_*}{\mu_{\tau, b}}_K = \dotprod{\mu_*}{\ \sum_{j=1}^{p_\epsilon} \beta_j K(t_j, \cdot)}_K = \sum_{j=1}^{p_\epsilon} \beta_j \dotprod{\mu_*}{K(t_j, \cdot)}_K = \mu_*(\tau)'b.
\]
Thus, using the familiar identity \(\|f-g\|^2 = \|f\|^2 + \|g\|^2 - 2\dotprod{f}{g}\), we can write
\[
f(\tau, b) = \dotprod{\mu_*}{\mu_{\tau, b}}_K - \frac{1}{2}\|\mu_{\tau,b}\|^2_K = \frac{1}{2}\|\mu_*\|^2_K - \frac{1}{2}\|\mu_* - \mu_{\tau, b}\|^2_K.
\]
Moreover, by the triangle inequality we have
\[
\|\mu_* - \mu_{\tau, b}\|_K \leq \|\mu_* - \mu_{\tau_\epsilon, b_\epsilon}\|_K + \|\mu_{\tau_\epsilon, b_\epsilon} - \mu_{\tau,b}\|_K.
\]
Then, by~\eqref{eq:approximation-property-mu} and the continuity of the map \((\tau, b) \mapsto \mu_{\tau, b}\) in the \acs{RKHS} norm (which follows from the continuity of \(K\)), after shrinking \(U\) if necessary we have \(\|\mu_* - \mu_{\tau,b}\|_K < 2\epsilon\) for all \((\tau,b)\in U\). Consequently,
\[
f(\tau, b) \geq \frac{1}{2}\|\mu_*\|^2_K - 2\epsilon^2 =: c_\epsilon > 0,
\]
where positivity holds by choosing \(\epsilon\) small enough. On the other hand, note that by the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality
\[
\sqrt{n} Z_\tau'b \geq \sqrt{n}\cdot \inf_{b\in U_2} Z_\tau'b \geq -\sqrt{n}\cdot \sup_{b\in U_2} \|b\|\cdot \sup_{\tau \in U_1}\|Z_\tau\|.
\]

All in all, we see from~\eqref{eq:log-likelihood-H1} that for all \((\tau, b)\in U\) we obtain a uniform lower bound for the log-likelihood given by
\[
\log L(X_{1:n}\mid p_\epsilon, \tau, b) = nf(\tau, b) +  \sqrt{n}Z_\tau'b \geq nc_\epsilon - \sqrt{n}B\cdot \sup_{\tau\in U_1}\|Z_\tau\|,
\]
where \(B=\sup_{b\in U_2} \|b\|\) satisfies \(0 < B < \infty\), since \(U_2\) is a compact set with \(0\notin U_2\). Hence, by~\eqref{eq:posterior-slab-m1} and~\eqref{eq:m-slab}, we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lower-bound-m1}
  \begin{split}
  m_1(X_{1:n}) &\geq \Pi(p_\epsilon) \int_{U_1\times U_2}\Pi_{\tau,b}^{\text{slab}}(\tau, b\mid p_\epsilon) \exp\left\{nf(\tau, b) +  \sqrt{n}Z_\tau'b\right\}db\, d\tau \\
  &\geq D \exp\left\{nc_\epsilon - \sqrt{n}B\cdot \sup_{\tau\in U_1}\|Z_\tau\|\right\},
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
where \(D=\Pi(p_\epsilon)\Pi_\tau(U_1|p_{\epsilon})\Pi_b^{\text{slab}}(U_2|p_{\epsilon})>0\) by assumption. Next, note that \(\sup_{\tau\in U_1}\|Z_\tau\|\) is \(O_{\pp_{\mu_*}}(1)\) (by the same argument as in the proof of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-rates-h0}), so for all \(\delta>0\) there exists \(M>0\) such that \(\pp_{\mu_*}(B\cdot \sup_{\tau\in U_1} \|Z_\tau\| \geq M) < \delta\). Thus, by~\eqref{eq:lower-bound-m1} we get
\[
\pp_{\mu_*}(m_1(X_{1:n}) \leq D \exp\{nc_\epsilon - \sqrt{n}M\}) \leq  \pp_{\mu_*}\left(B\cdot \sup_{\tau\in U_1} \|Z_\tau\| \geq M\right) < \delta.
\]
Since \(nc_\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)\) grows slower than \(nc_\epsilon - \sqrt{n}M\) as \(n\to\infty\), for sufficiently large \(n\) we get the probability bound
\begin{align*}
&\pp_{\mu_*}\left(m_1(X_{1:n}) \leq D \exp\left\{\frac{nc_\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}\right\}\right)\\
 &\quad \leq \pp_{\mu_*}\left(m_1(X_{1:n})\leq D \exp\{nc_\epsilon - \sqrt{n}M\}\right) < \delta.
\end{align*}
To conclude, observe that by~\eqref{eq:posterior-slab}
\[
\Pi_n(H_0\mid X_{1:n}) = \frac{\pi_0}{\pi_0 + (1-\pi_0)m_1(X_{1:n})} \leq \frac{\pi_0}{(1-\pi_0)m_1(X_{1:n})},
\]
so if we define \(\tilde{D}=\pi_0/((1-\pi_0)D)\), for large \(n\) we have
\begin{align*}
&\pp_{\mu_*}\left(\Pi_n(H_0\mid X_{1:n}) > \tilde{D}\exp\left\{\frac{-nc_\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}\right\}\right)\\
&\quad \leq \pp_{\mu_*}\left(m_1(X_{1:n}) \leq D \exp\left\{\frac{nc_\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}\right\}\right) < \delta,
\end{align*}
i.e., \(\Pi_n(H_0|X_{1:n}) = O_{\pp_{\mu_*}}(e^{-nc_\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)})\). Since \(\epsilon\) was arbitrarily small, the constant \(\gamma\) in the exponent is as close as desired to 
\[
  \lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\, \frac{c_\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}=\frac{1}{2}\|\mu_*\|^2_K.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-rates-h1-alt}]\label{proof:consistency-rates-h1-alt}
  By~\ref{ass:positive-prior-mass-alt}, there exist \(p_0\leq P_{\max}\), \(\tau_0\in \mathcal C_{p_0}\), and \(b_0\in\R^{p_0}\setminus\{0\}\) such that
  \[
  \xi:=\|\mu_*-\mu_{\tau_0,b_0}\|_K < \|\mu_*\|_K.
  \]
  By the full support condition in~\ref{ass:positive-prior-mass-alt}, there exists a compact neighborhood \(U=U_1\times U_2\) of \((\tau_0,b_0)\) within \(\mathcal C_{p_0}\times\R^{p_0}\setminus\{0\}\) with 
  \[
    \Pi(p_0)\Pi_\tau(U_1\mid p_0)\Pi_b^{\text{slab}}(U_2\mid p_0)>0.
  \]
  Let \((\tau,b)\in U\) and define \(f(\tau, b)\) as in the proof of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-rates-h1}, i.e.,
  \[
  f(\tau, b) = \frac{1}{2}\|\mu_*\|_K^2 - \frac{1}{2}\|\mu_* - \mu_{\tau, b}\|_K^2.
  \]
  Let \(\epsilon >0\) be small enough so that \(\xi + \epsilon < \|\mu_*\|_K\). By shrinking \(U\) we can ensure that \(\|\mu_{\tau_0,b_0}-\mu_{\tau,b}\|_K < \epsilon\), and then by the triangle inequality we have \(\|\mu_*-\mu_{\tau,b}\|_K \leq \xi + \epsilon\). Therefore,
  \[
  f(\tau, b) \geq \frac{1}{2}\|\mu_*\|_K^2 - \frac{1}{2}(\xi + \epsilon)^2 =: c_{\epsilon,\xi} > 0 \quad\text{for all }(\tau,b)\in U,
  \]
  where positivity holds because \(\xi + \epsilon < \|\mu_*\|_K\). From this point on, we proceed exactly as in the proof of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-rates-h1}, obtaining the rate \(\Pi_n(H_0|X_{1:n})=O_{\pp_\mu*}(e^{-nc_{\epsilon,\xi}/(1+\epsilon)})\). Letting \(\epsilon\to 0\) gives a constant \(\gamma\) in the exponent as close as desired to \(\frac{1}{2}(\|\mu_*\|_K^2 - \xi^2)\).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Consistency and contraction rates for plug-in covariance}

Firstly, note that under~\ref{ass:continuous-paths} the empirical covariance estimator \(\hat C_n\) is uniformly consistent almost surely, i.e., 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uniform-consistency-empirical-covariance}
\|\hat C_n - K\|_\infty \stackrel{\text{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow} 0\quad (n\to\infty).
\end{equation}
This follows directly from Mourier's law of large numbers in Banach spaces \citep[Theorem 7.2.1]{laha1979probability} because \(\E[\|X\|_\infty^2] < \infty\). To establish the finiteness of the second moment under the supremum norm, \citet{landau1970supremum} showed that \(\E[e^{\epsilon \|X\|_\infty^2}] < \infty\) for sufficiently small \(\epsilon>0\), which immediately implies \(\E[\|X\|_\infty^2] < \infty\).

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:sample-cov-matrix-convergence}]\label{proof:sample-cov-matrix-convergence}
  The first assertion in (i) is a consequence of the scalar law of large numbers applied entrywise. Alternatively, it can be immediately deduced from~\eqref{eq:uniform-consistency-empirical-covariance} by choosing the max-norm on matrices (since all matrix norms are equivalent):
  \[
  \|\hat{\mathbf C}_n - \mathbf K\|_{\max} = \max_{1\leq g,h\leq G} |\hat C_n(s_g, s_h) - K(s_g, s_h)| \leq \|\hat C_n - K\|_\infty \stackrel{\text{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow} 0.
  \]
  The second assertion is proven analogously.

  For (ii), note that \(\tr(\cdot)\) is continuous, so from (i) we have
  \[
  \tr(\hat{\mathbf C}_n) \stackrel{\text{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow} \tr(\mathbf K)\quad \text{and}\quad \tr(\hat{\mathbf C}_n^2) \stackrel{\text{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow} \tr(\mathbf K^2).
  \]
  Then, the numerator in \(\hat\rho_n\), defined in~\eqref{eq:rho-oas}, converges almost surely to a finite positive constant (as long as \(G\geq 2\)). For the denominator, observe that
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:rho-oas-denominator-convergence}
    \tr(\hat{\mathbf C}_n^2) - \frac{1}{G}\tr^2(\hat{\mathbf C}_n) \stackrel{\text{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow} \tr(\mathbf K^2) - \frac{1}{G}\tr^2(\mathbf K).
  \end{equation}
  By Cauchy--Schwarz (using the definition of the trace as the sum of the eigenvalues), the right-hand side of~\eqref{eq:rho-oas-denominator-convergence} is always nonnegative, and it vanishes if and only if the true covariance matrix \(\mathbf K\) is spherical. Thus, we consider two cases:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item If, say, \(\mathbf K =\sigma^2 I_G\) with \(\sigma^2>0\), then the behavior of \(\hat \rho_n\) as \(n\to\infty\) is not easily determined (though it can be analyzed by a central limit theorem argument). Luckily this is irrelevant, since now both the empirical covariance and target covariance matrices converge a.s.\ to the same limit, namely \(\mathbf K\). Then,
  \[
  \|\hat{\mathbf K}_n^{\text{OAS}} - \mathbf K\| \leq (1-\hat\rho_n) \|\hat{\mathbf C}_n - \mathbf K\| + \hat\rho_n \left\|\frac{\tr(\hat{\mathbf C}_n)}{G}I_G - \mathbf K\right\| \stackrel{\text{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow} 0.
  \]
  \item If \(\mathbf K\) is not spherical, then \(\|\tr(\hat{\mathbf C}_n)I_G/G - \mathbf K\|\) converges to a finite constant almost surely. On the other hand, the denominator in~\eqref{eq:rho-oas} diverges almost surely as \(n\to\infty\), so \(\hat\rho_n\stackrel{\text{a.s.}}{\to} 0\). Then, using the same upper bound as in the previous case, we also get \(\|\hat{\mathbf K}_n^{\text{OAS}} - \mathbf K\|\to 0\) a.s.
  \end{itemize}

  We now prove the second assertion in (ii) in detail. The case \(p=1\) is trivial, so we assume \(p>1\). By the triangle inequality and particularizing to the Frobenius norm \(\|\cdot\|_F\), we have
  \[
  \sup_{\tau\in \mathcal C_p}\|\hat K^{\text{OAS}}_{n,\tau} - K_\tau\|_F \leq \sup_{\tau\in \mathcal C_p}\|\hat C_{n,\tau} - K_\tau\|_F + \sup_{\tau\in \mathcal C_p}\hat\rho_{n,\tau}\left\|\frac{\tr(\hat C_{n,\tau})}{p}I_p - \hat C_{n,\tau}\right\|_F.
  \]
  The first term on the right-hand side goes to 0 almost surely by the uniform convergence established in (i). For the second term, let
  \[
  \hat{D}_{n,\tau} = \frac{\tr(\hat C_{n,\tau})}{p}I_p - \hat C_{n,\tau},
  \]
  and note that \(\|\hat{D}_{n,\tau}\|_F^2 = \tr(\hat D_{n,\tau}\hat D_{n,\tau}') = \tr(\hat C_{n,\tau}^2) - \tr^2(\hat C_{n,\tau})/p\). Then, for \(\tau\in \mathcal C_p\) we have
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:rho-frobenius-difference}
  \hat\rho_{n,\tau}\left\|\frac{\tr(\hat C_{n,\tau})}{p}I_p - \hat C_{n,\tau}\right\|_F = \min\left\{1,\, \frac{\hat{N}_{n,\tau}}{(n + 1 - 2/p)\|\hat{D}_{n,\tau}\|_F^2}\right\}\|\hat{D}_{n,\tau}\|_F,
  \end{equation}
  where \(\hat{N}_{n,\tau} = (1-2/p) \tr(\hat C_{n,\tau}^2) + \tr^2(\hat C_{n,\tau}) \geq 0\). 

  Now, for any \(A\geq 0\), \(c>0\) and \(x\geq 0\) the algebraic inequality \(\min\left\{1,\frac{A}{cx^2}\right\}x \leq \sqrt{\frac{A}{c}}\) holds (if \(x \leq \sqrt{A/c}\) the bound is trivial since the minimum is at most one; if \(x > \sqrt{A/c}\) it evaluates to \(\frac{A}{cx^2}\), and in turn \(\frac{A}{cx^2}x < \sqrt{A/c}\)). Applying this bound to~\eqref{eq:rho-frobenius-difference} yields
  \[
  \hat\rho_{n,\tau}\|\hat D_{n,\tau}\|_F \leq \sqrt{\frac{\hat{N}_{n,\tau}}{n + 1 - 2/p}}.
  \]

  To conclude, we show that \(\hat N_{n,\tau}\) is uniformly bounded almost surely for large \(n\). To see this, define the continuous map \(F:\R^{p\times p} \to \R\) given by \(F(A)= (1-2/p)\tr(A^2)+\tr^2(A)\). Note that the set \(\{K_\tau: \tau \in \mathcal C_p\}\) is compact, so uniform convergence of \(\hat C_{n,\tau}\) to \(K_\tau\), given by (i), implies that for large \(n\) all \(\hat C_{n,\tau}\) lie almost surely in a fixed compact neighborhood of this set. The function \(F\) is uniformly continuous on this compact neighborhood, and therefore 
  \[
  \sup_{\tau\in \mathcal C_p}|F(\hat C_{n,\tau}) - F(K_\tau)| = \sup_{\tau\in \mathcal C_p}|\hat N_{n,\tau} - F(K_\tau)| \stackrel{\text{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow} 0.
  \] 
  This implies that \(\sup_{\tau\in \mathcal C_p}\hat N_{n,\tau}\leq \sup_{\tau\in \mathcal C_p} F(K_\tau)+ 1 =: M\) a.s.\ for all \(n\) large enough, and \(M<\infty\) by continuity. Hence,
  \[
  \sup_{\tau \in \mathcal C_p} \hat\rho_{n,\tau}\|\hat D_{n,\tau}\|_F \leq \sqrt{\frac{M}{n + 1 - 2/p}} \stackrel{\text{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow} 0.
  \]
\end{proof}

We now prove the plug-in consistency results for our test. Define the plug-in marginal likelihood
\[
\hat \mslab(X_{1:n}\mid p,\tau) = \eta^{-p}|\hat\Lambda_{n,\tau}|^{-1/2}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{2}S_n(\tau)'\hat\Lambda_{n,\tau}^{-1}S_n(\tau)\right\},
\]
where \(\hat\Lambda_{n,\tau} = n\hat K_{n,\tau} + \eta^{-2}I_p \). Similarly, consider the plug-in posterior probability of the alternative
\[
\hat\Pi_n(H_1\mid X_{1:n}) = \frac{(1-\pi_0)\hat m_1(X_{1:n})}{\pi_0 + (1-\pi_0)\hat m_1(X_{1:n})},
\]
where
\[
\hat m_1(X_{1:n}) = \sum_{p\geq 1}\Pi(p)\int_{\mathcal T_p}\hat \mslab(X_{1:n}\mid p,\tau)\Pi(\tau\mid p)\,d\tau.
\]

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-rates-h0-plugin}]\label{proof:consistency-rates-h0-plugin}
  We will follow the proof of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-rates-h0} and adapt it only where necessary. First, observe that by~\ref{ass:compact-support-tau} and~\ref{ass:prior-p-finite} the sum \(\hat m_1(X_{1:n})\) reduces to
\[
\hat m_1(X_{1:n}) = \sum_{p=p_{\min}}^{P_{\max}} \Pi(p)\,\hat I_{n,p}(\mathcal C_p),
\]
where \(\hat I_{n,p}(\mathcal C_p)=\int_{\mathcal C_p}\hat \mslab(X_{1:n}|p,\tau)\Pi(\tau|p)\,d\tau\). Fix \(p_{\min} \leq p\leq P_{\max}\), and recall that there exists \(\lambda_p>0\) with \(\lambda_{\min}(K_\tau)\geq \lambda_p\) for all \(\tau\in \mathcal C_p\). By Weyl's inequality, choosing the spectral norm \(\|\cdot\|_2\) on matrices, we have
\[
\lambda_{\min}(\hat K_{n,\tau}) \geq \lambda_{\min}(K_\tau) - \|\hat K_{n,\tau} - K_\tau\|_2 \geq \lambda_p - \sup_{\tau\in \mathcal C_p}\|\hat K_{n,\tau} - K_\tau\|_2.
\]
Define the event where \(\hat K_{n,\tau}\) is uniformly close to \(K_\tau\) with a suitable bound, i.e.,
\[
F_n = \left\{\sup_{\tau\in \mathcal C_p}\|\hat K_{n,\tau} - K_\tau\|_2 \leq \frac{\lambda_p}{2}\right\},
\]
and note that \(\pp_0(F_n^c)\to 0\) as \(n\to\infty\) by~\ref{ass:uniform-consistency-plug-in}. Furthermore, on \(F_n\) we have \(\lambda_{\min}(\hat K_{n,\tau})\geq \lambda_p/2\), and consequently \(\lambda_{\min}(\hat\Lambda_{n,\tau})\geq n\lambda_p/2>0\) for all \(\tau\in \mathcal C_p\). This yields the upper bound on \(F_n\)
\begin{equation}\label{eq:upper-bound-m-slab-plugin}
\hat \mslab(X_{1:n}\mid p,\tau) \leq \eta^{-p}\left(\frac{n\lambda_p}{2}\right)^{-p/2}\exp\left\{\frac{\|S_n(\tau)\|^2}{n\lambda_p}\right\}, \quad \text{for all }\tau\in \mathcal C_p.
\end{equation}

Let \(\epsilon>0\) and define the event \(E_{M}\) for a large enough \(M\) as in~\eqref{eq:event-E-n-M}, so that \(\pp_0(E_M^c)<\epsilon/2\). On the high-probability event \(F_n\cap E_{M}\), incorporating~\eqref{eq:upper-bound-m-slab-plugin} gives after integration
\[
\hat I_{n,p}(\mathcal C_p) \leq \eta^{-p}\left(\frac{n\lambda_p}{2}\right)^{-p/2}\exp\left\{\frac{M}{\lambda_p}\right\} =: \hat D\, n^{-p/2},
\]
where \(\hat D=\eta^{-p}(\lambda_p/2)^{-p/2}\exp\{M/\lambda_p\}>0\) is a finite constant independent of \(n\). Then, for sufficiently large \(n\),
\[
\pp_0(\hat I_{n,p}(\mathcal C_p)>\hat D\, n^{-p/2}) \leq \pp_0(F_n^c)+\pp_0(E_{M}^c) < \epsilon,
\]
showing \(\hat I_{n,p}(\mathcal C_p)=O_{\pp_0}(n^{-p/2})\). Then, we conclude exactly as in the proof of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-rates-h0}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-rates-h1-plugin-1}]\label{proof:consistency-rates-h1-plugin-1}
We follow the proof strategy of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-rates-h1}. Recover the approximating point \((p_\epsilon,\tau_\epsilon,b_\epsilon)\), the compact neighborhood \(U=U_1\times U_2\) of \((\tau_\epsilon, b_\epsilon)\) with positive prior probability, and the function \(f(\tau, b)=\mu_*(\tau)'b - \frac{1}{2}b'K_\tau b\), which satisfies \(f(\tau, b)\geq \frac{1}{2}\|\mu_*\|_K^2 - 2\epsilon^2 >0\) for all \((\tau,b)\in U\) and sufficiently small \(\epsilon>0\). Define the plug-in analogue
\[
\hat f_n(\tau,b) = \mu_*(\tau)'b - \frac{1}{2}b'\hat K_{n,\tau} b,\quad \text{for all } (\tau,b)\in U,
\]
and bound the plug-in error by
\begin{align*}
\sup_{(\tau,b)\in U}|\hat f_n(\tau,b) - f(\tau,b)| &= \sup_{(\tau,b)\in U}\frac{1}{2}|b'(\hat K_{n,\tau}-K_\tau)b| \\
&\leq \frac{B^2}{2}\sup_{\tau\in U_1}\|\hat K_{n,\tau}-K_\tau\|_2,
\end{align*}
where \(0<B=\sup_{b\in U_2} \|b\|<\infty\) and \(\|\cdot\|_2\) is the spectral norm on matrices. Now, define the event 
\[
G_n = \left\{\sup_{\tau\in U_1}\|\hat K_{n,\tau} - K_\tau\|_2 \leq \frac{2\epsilon^2}{B^2}\right\}.
\]
On the one hand, by~\ref{ass:uniform-consistency-plug-in} we have \(\pp_{\mu_*}(G_n^c)\to 0\) as \(n\to\infty\). On the other hand, on \(G_n\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:plugin-f-lower-bound}
\hat f_n(\tau,b) \geq f(\tau,b) - \epsilon^2 \geq \frac{1}{2}\|\mu_*\|_K^2 - 3\epsilon^2 =: d_\epsilon, \quad \text{for all } (\tau,b)\in U,
\end{equation}
where \(d_\epsilon>0\) can be achieved by taking \(\epsilon\) small enough. In this case, the plug-in log-likelihood for \(p_\epsilon\) and \((\tau, b)\in U\) becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:log-likelihood-plugin}
  \begin{split}
  \log \hat L(X_{1:n}\mid p_\epsilon, \tau, b) &= n\mu_*(\tau)'b - \frac{n}{2}b'\hat K_{n,\tau}b + \sqrt{n}Z_\tau' b \\
  &= n\hat f_n(\tau, b) + \sqrt{n}Z_\tau' b,
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
where \(Z_\tau = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(S_n(\tau) - n\mu_*(\tau))\). Moreover, recall that by Cauchy--Schwarz we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cauchy-schwarz-plugin}
\sqrt{n} Z_\tau' b \geq -\sqrt{n} B \sup_{\tau \in U_1}\|Z_\tau\|.
\end{equation}

On \(G_n\), restricting the domain of integration and using~\eqref{eq:plugin-f-lower-bound},~\eqref{eq:log-likelihood-plugin} and~\eqref{eq:cauchy-schwarz-plugin} produces
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lower-bound-m1-plugin}
  \begin{split}
  \hat m_1(X_{1:n}) &\geq \Pi(p_\epsilon)\int_{U_1 \times U_2}\Pi_{\tau, b}^{\text{slab}}(\tau, b \mid p_\epsilon)\exp\left\{n\hat f_n(\tau,b)+\sqrt{n} Z_\tau'b\right\} db\,d\tau \\
  &\geq D\exp\left\{nd_\epsilon - \sqrt{n}\,B\cdot\sup_{\tau\in U_1}\|Z_\tau\|\right\},
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
where \(D=\Pi(p_\epsilon)\Pi_\tau(U_1|p_{\epsilon})\Pi_b^{\text{slab}}(U_2|p_{\epsilon})>0\) by assumption. Now, since \(Z_\tau \sim \mathcal N_{p_\epsilon}(0, K_\tau)\) under \(H_1\), we already saw in the proof of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-rates-h1} that for any \(\delta>0\) there exists \(M>0\) such that the event
\[
H_M = \left\{B\cdot \sup_{\tau\in U_1}\|Z_\tau\| \leq M\right\}
\]
satisfies \(\pp_{\mu_*}(H_M^c)<\delta/2\). Thus, by~\eqref{eq:lower-bound-m1-plugin} we have the inequality 
\[
  \hat m_1(X_{1:n}) \geq D\exp\{nd_\epsilon - \sqrt{n}M\}\quad \text{on }G_n\cap H_M.
\]
Since \(nd_\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)\) grows slower than \(nd_\epsilon - \sqrt{n}M\) as \(n\to\infty\), for sufficiently large \(n\) we obtain 
\[
  \hat m_1(X_{1:n}) \geq D\exp\{n d_\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)\}\quad \text{on } G_n\cap H_M.
\]
Therefore,
\[
  \hat\Pi_n(H_0\mid X_{1:n}) = \frac{\pi_0}{(1-\pi_0)\hat m_1(X_{1:n})}\leq \tilde{D}\exp\left\{\frac{-nd_\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}\right\},\quad \text{on } G_n\cap H_M,
\]
where \(\tilde{D}=\pi_0/((1-\pi_0)D)\). Note that 
\[
  \pp_{\mu_*}(G_n^c\cup H_M^c)\leq \pp_{\mu_*}(G_n^c)+\pp_{\mu_*}(H_M^c),
\]
and observe that the first term vanishes as \(n\to\infty\), while the second is at most \(\delta/2\). Then, for sufficiently large \(n\) we have proved that
\[
\pp_{\mu_*}\left(\hat\Pi_n(H_0\mid X_{1:n})>\tilde{D}\exp\{-nd_\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)\}\right)<\delta,
\]
so \(\hat\Pi_n(H_0|X_{1:n})=O_{\pp_{\mu_*}}(e^{-nd_\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)})\) and, as before, 
\[
  \lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\, d_\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)=\frac{1}{2}\|\mu_*\|_K^2.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-rates-h1-plugin-2}]\label{proof:consistency-rates-h1-plugin-2}
  The proof is almost identical to the proof of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-rates-h1-plugin-1}, except that \(f(\tau,b)\) is lower bounded by \(c_{\epsilon,\xi}\) as in the construction from the proof of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-rates-h1-alt}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{On optimality of the contraction rates}

We conclude with a proof of the information-theoretic optimality of the contraction rate in Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-rates-h1}. We will need the following lemma, which is just the standard fact that marginal likelihoods are densities with respect to the fixed reference measure.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:expectation-identity} For any \((p, \tau)\in \N \times \mathcal T_p\) and every \(n\geq 1\), we have 
  \[
    \E_{\pp_0}[m_{\emph{slab}}(X_{1:n}\mid p,\tau)] = 1.
  \]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  It follows directly from Fubini/Tonelli and the properties of Radon--Nikodym derivatives:
  \begin{align*}
    \E_{\pp_0}[\mslab(X_{1:n}\mid p,\tau)] &= \bigintsss \E_{\pp_0}\biggl[\frac{d\pp_{\mu_\theta}}{d\pp_0}(X_{1:n})\biggr]\Pi^{\text{slab}}(b)\, db\\
    &= \int \Pi^{\text{slab}}(b)\, db = 1.
  \end{align*}
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:optimality-rates-h1}]\label{proof:optimality-rates-h1}
  Suppose by contradiction that a faster rate holds, say \(\Pi_n(H_0|X_{1:n})=O_{\pp_{\mu_*}}(e^{-n\zeta})\) for \(\zeta > \frac{1}{2}\|\mu_*\|_K^2\). By definition, for any \(0<\epsilon<1/2\) there exists \(M>0\) such that the region
  \[
  R_n = \{X_{1:n}: \Pi_n(H_0\mid X_{1:n}) \leq M e^{-n\zeta}\}
  \]
  satisfies \(\pp_{\mu_*}(R_n)> 1-\epsilon\) for sufficiently large \(n\). Then, reusing the notation \(m_1(X_{1:n})\) from~\eqref{eq:posterior-slab-m1}, on \(R_n\) we have 
  \[
  \Pi_n(H_0\mid X_{1:n}) = \frac{\pi_0}{\pi_0 + (1-\pi_0)m_1(X_{1:n})} \leq M e^{-n\zeta},
  \]
  and rearranging yields
  \[
  \pi_0(1-M e^{-n\zeta}) \leq (1-\pi_0)m_1(X_{1:n}) M e^{-n\zeta}.
  \]
  For sufficiently large \(n\) we have \(1-M e^{-n\zeta} \geq 1/2\), so eventually we get
  \[
  1\leq \frac{2M(1-\pi_0)}{\pi_0} e^{-n\zeta} m_1(X_{1:n}).
  \]
  Integrating on \(R_n\) on both sides with respect to \(\pp_0\) and using the fact that \(m_1(X_{1:n})\) is a valid marginal likelihood (it integrates to one under \(\pp_0\) over the whole space by Lemma~\ref{lem:expectation-identity}), we have
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:type-ii-error-bound}
  \pp_0(R_n) \leq \frac{2M(1-\pi_0)}{\pi_0} e^{-n\zeta} \int_{R_n} m_1(X_{1:n})\, d\pp_0 \leq C e^{-n\zeta}\quad \text{for large } n,
  \end{equation}
  where \(C>0\) is a constant. Now we construct a frequentist test \(\phi_n\) for the reverse hypotheses \(\tilde{H}_0: \mu\equiv \mu_*\) against \(\tilde{H}_1: \mu\equiv 0\), given by \(\phi_n(X_{1:n}) = \mathds{1}\{X_{1:n} \in R_n^c\}\). In this case, according to~\eqref{eq:rn-derivative} the Kullback--Leibler divergence from \(\pp_{\mu_*}\) to \(\pp_0\) is
  \[
  \dkl(\pp_{\mu_*} \,\|\, \pp_0) = \E_{\pp_{\mu_*}}[\dotprod{X}{\mu_*}_K] - \frac{1}{2}\|\mu_*\|^2_K = \frac{1}{2}\|\mu_*\|_K^2,
  \]
  where we used the equivalence \(\E_{\pp_{\mu_*}}[\dotprod{X}{\mu_*}_K]=\dotprod{\mu_*}{\mu_*}_K=\|\mu_*\|_K^2\) that stems from Loève's isometry \citep[p.~974]{parzen1961approach}. Now, since \(\pp_{\mu_*}(\phi_n = 1)=\pp_{\mu_*}(R_n^c) < \epsilon\), the type I error probability is bounded for sufficiently large \(n\). On the other hand, by~\eqref{eq:type-ii-error-bound} the type II error probability for large \(n\) is \(\pp_0(\phi_n=0)=\pp_0(R_n)\leq C e^{-n\zeta}\), which decays exponentially with exponential constant greater than \(\dkl(\pp_{\mu_*} \,\|\, \pp_0)\), contradicting Chernoff--Stein.
\end{proof}
