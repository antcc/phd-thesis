\noindent A core problem in statistics involves hypothesis testing for quantities of interest. As such, considerable efforts have been devoted to generalizing frequentist two-sample tests~\citep{zhang2010two,fan1998test,hall2007two}, analysis of variance methods~\citep{cuevas2004anova, cuesta2010simple, zhang2013analysis}, change point detection procedures~\citep{berkes2009detecting,hormann2010weakly}, repeated measures analysis~\citep{martinez2011repeated,smaga2020note} or dependence tests in linear regression~\citep{kokoszka2008testing,cardot2003testing} to functional data, providing \(p\)-values and decision rules based on asymptotic approximations or bootstrap resampling. However, the conceptually simpler one-sample tests, often used to detect the presence or absence of a certain effect within a given population, have received less attention in the FDA literature. Notable exceptions include \citet{zhang2013analysis,mas2007testing, munk2008one}, where the authors explore different tests for the mean of a functional sample.

On the other hand, Bayesian methods for hypothesis testing---introduced in modern form by \citet{jeffreys1961theory}---are extensively studied and applied in multivariate settings, offering a principled alternative to the often criticized \(p\)-value approach~\citep[see][and references therein for a general overview]{robert202250,robert2007bayesian}. However, despite their broad adoption, they remain comparatively underdeveloped within the FDA framework. We aim to bridge this gap by proposing a Bayesian test for the mean function \(\mu\) of a Gaussian process observed through i.i.d.\ samples, which serves as a building block for more complex statistical analyses. In particular, we provide a method for testing the point-null hypothesis \(H_0: \mu \equiv \mu_0\) versus the composite alternative \(H_1: \mu \not\equiv \mu_0\), where \(\mu_0(\cdot)\) is a predetermined function.\footnote{We focus on the simple hypothesis of exact equality, in contrast to \textit{precise} or \textit{relevant} hypotheses such as \(H_0^\epsilon: \|\mu-\mu_0\| < \epsilon\) for a given \(\epsilon>0\). Although these precise hypothesis may be useful when an exact zero is not meaningful or reasonable, in a Bayesian framework they can be conceptually encoded in the corresponding point-null hypothesis via prior beliefs, as argued by \citet{berger1987testing}.} The test is based on the Bayes factor
\[
\bff_{10} = \frac{\pp(\text{data}|H_1)}{\pp(\text{data}|H_0)},
\]
which measures the evidence for \(H_1\) over \(H_0\) and is linked to the prior and posterior odds through the formula
\[
\frac{\pp(H_1|\text{data})}{\pp(H_0|\text{data})} = \bff_{10} \, \times\, \frac{\pp(H_1)}{\pp(H_0)}.
\]
Using Bayes factors to quantify the evidence against the null hypothesis in a continuous manner is the standard approach in Bayesian testing, popularized since the seminal work by \citet{kass1995bayes}. Because they are intimately related to the posterior probability of the null, Bayes factors provide a direct probabilistic way of interpreting the test decision, while also allowing the incorporation of prior information. Moreover, despite being a loose analog of a likelihood ratio test, the Bayes factor \(\bff_{10}\) can be inverted to provide evidence in favor of \(H_0\), which is not possible in frequentist tests due to the built-in asymmetry. 

Most functional tests in the literature employ some form of dimension reduction via projections or basis expansions as a first step. Following earlier work~\citep[e.g.][]{berrendero2018use,berrendero2025bayesian} we take a slightly different approach and directly assume that the mean function is a member of the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) associated with the covariance function of the underlying Gaussian process, so that it can be approximated via a finite linear combination of kernel sections. We will see that this assumption is reasonable and allows us to completely specify a Bayesian test, circumventing the difficulty of placing a prior distribution on an infinite-dimensional space, and providing a closed-form likelihood expression via Radon-Nikodym derivatives. Since we do not restrict ourselves to conjugate models, we use the reversible jump variant of Markov chain Monte Carlo methods (RJMCMC), proposed by \citet{green1995reversible}, to approximate the posterior distribution. From this approximation we obtain estimators of the posterior probability of the null hypothesis and the Bayes factor, which can be used to guide decision-making while explicitly accounting for uncertainty.

To validate the methodology from a theoretical point of view, we study the large-sample consistency of our test and measure the speed of posterior concentration. This technique has become a standard tool in the frequentist assessment of Bayesian procedures, as exemplified by the highly relevant work of \citet{ghosal2017fundamentals}. We show that under \(H_0\) the posterior probability of the alternative goes to zero at a polynomial rate, while under \(H_1\) the posterior probability of the null decays exponentially fast. On the other hand, finite-sample performance is evaluated by numerical simulations in terms of frequentist metrics such as type I error (false positive rate) and power (true positive rate).
