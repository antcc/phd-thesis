\noindent Our first goal is to study functional regression problems with scalar response---the infinite-dimensional equivalents of the typical statistical regression problems---from a Bayesian perspective. In particular, we are concerned with functional linear and logistic regression models, that is, situations where the objective is to predict a continuous or dichotomous variable from functional observations. To set a common framework, we will consider throughout a scalar response variable \(Y\) (either continuous or binary) which has some dependence on the stochastic process \(X\). We will further suppose for simplicity that \(X\) is centered, that is, \(\E[X(t)]=0\) for all \(t\in[0,1]\). In addition, we will tacitly assume the existence of a labeled data set \(\mathcal D_n =\{(X_i, Y_i): i=1,\dots, n\}\) of independent observations from \((X, Y)\), and our aim will be to accurately predict the response corresponding to unlabeled functional samples from \(X\).

It turns out that in both regression scenarios a natural alternative to the \(L^2\)-model described in Section~\ref{sec:functional-regression} is the so-called \acs{RKHS} model, which instead assumes the unknown functional parameter \(\beta\) to be a member of the \acs{RKHS} associated with the covariance function of the process \(X\). As we will show later on, not only is this model simpler and arguably easier to interpret, but it also constrains the parameter space to smoother and more manageable functions. In fact, it does include a model based on finite linear combinations of the marginals of \(X\) as a particular case, while also generalizing the aforementioned \(L^2\)-models under some conditions. These \acs{RKHS}-based models and their idiosyncrasies have been explored in \citet{berrendero2019rkhs, berrendero2024functional} in the linear case, and in \citet{berrendero2023functional} in the logistic case.

Our main contribution is the proposal of a Bayesian approach for inference in these \acs{RKHS} models, in which a prior distribution is imposed on \(\beta\) to use the posterior probabilities for prediction. Similar Bayesian schemes have recently been explored in \citet{grollemund2019bayesian} and \citet{abraham2024informative}, albeit not in a \acs{RKHS} setting. Another set of techniques extensively studied in this context are variable selection methods, which aim to select the marginals \(\{X(t_j)\}\) of the process that better summarize it according to some optimality criterion (see \citealp{ferraty2010most} or \citealp{berrendero2016variable} by way of illustration). As it happens, some \acs{RKHS}-based variable selection methods have already been proposed \citep[e.g.][]{bueno2019variable}, but in general they have their own dedicated algorithms and procedures. As will shortly become apparent, our Bayesian methodology allows us to easily isolate the marginal posterior distribution corresponding to a finite set of marginals, providing a Bayesian variable selection process along with other prediction methods that naturally arise from our approach.

\section{Reproducing kernel models for functional regression}

In principle, the functional \acs{RKHS} models contemplated in this work are those obtained by considering a functional parameter \(\beta \in \Hcal(K)\) and replacing the scalar product for \(\dotprod{X}{\beta}_K\) in the \(L^2\)-models~\eqref{eq:l2-linear-model} and~\eqref{eq:l2-logistic-model}, which has tangible benefits both in theory and practice on account of the \acs{RKHS} properties. However, to further simplify things we will follow a parametric approach and suppose that \(\beta\) is in fact a member of the dense subspace \(\Hcal_0(K)\) defined in~\eqref{eq:h0}, i.e.:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beta}
  \beta(\cdot) = \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j K(t_j, \cdot).
\end{equation}

In addition, as we said before, with a slight abuse of notation we will understand the expression \(\dotprod{x}{\beta}_K\) as \(\Psi_x(\beta)\), where \(x=X(\omega)\) is a realization of \(X\) and \(\Psi_x\) is Loève's isometry, defined in~\eqref{eq:loeves-isometry}. Hence, taking into account that \(\Psi_X(K(t, \cdot)) = X(t)\) by definition, we can write 
\[
  \dotprod{X}{\beta}_K := \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j X(t_j).
\]

Although fairly natural in this context, the crucial assumption that \(\beta \in \Hcal_0(K)\) may seem too restrictive at first glance, since we are essentially truncating the dimensionality of the model. Nevertheless, in this way we get a simpler, finite-dimensional approximation of the functional \acs{RKHS} model, which we argue reduces the overall complexity while still capturing most of the relevant information. Plus, the simplified model remains ``truly functional'' in the sense that the number of components \(p\) and the time instants \(t_j\) are not fixed beforehand.

In view of~\eqref{eq:beta}, to place a prior distribution on the unknown function \(\beta\) it suffices to consider a discrete prior distribution on the number of components \(p\), and then select \(p\)-dimensional continuous priors for the coefficients \(\beta_j\) and the times \(t_j\) given \(p\). Thanks to this parametric approach, the challenging task of setting a prior distribution on a space of functions is considerably simplified, while simultaneously not constraining the model to any specific distribution (in contrast to, say, Gaussian process regression methods). Moreover, note that our simplifying assumption on \(\beta\) is not particularly limiting from a Bayesian point of view, since any distribution \(\pp\) on \(\Hcal_0(K)\) can be directly extended to a distribution \(\bar{\pp}\) on \(\Hcal(K)\) by defining \(\bar{\pp}(B) = \pp(\Hcal_0(K)\cap B)\) for all Borel sets \(B\) on \(\Hcal(K)\).


\subsection{Functional linear regression}\label{sec:rkhs-linear-model}

In the case of functional linear regression, the simplified \acs{RKHS} model considered is
\[
  Y = \alpha + \dotprod{X}{\beta}_K + \epsilon = \alpha + \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j X(t_j) + \epsilon,
\]
where \(\beta(\cdot)=\sum_{j=1}^p\beta_j K(t_j, \cdot) \in \Hcal_0(K)\), \(\alpha\in\R\), and \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\) is an error term independent from \(X\). This model is essentially a finite-dimensional approximation from a functional perspective to the more general \acs{RKHS} model that assumes \(\beta \in \Hcal(K)\) \citep{berrendero2024functional}. Since the number of components \(p\) is unknown, a generic parameter will be denoted by \(\theta_p = (p, t_1,\dots, t_p, \beta_1,\dots, \beta_p, \alpha, \sigma^2) \equiv (p, \tau_p, b_p, \alpha, \sigma^2)\), though we will usually omit the subscript if the value of \(p\) is understood. The full parameter space is thus given by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:theta-parameter-space-bfr}
\Theta = \bigcup_{p\in\N}\Theta_p,\quad \text{where } \Theta_p = \left\{p\right\} \times [0, 1]^p \times \R^p  \times \R \times \R^+_0.
\end{equation}
For \(\theta \in \Theta\), the reinterpreted model in terms of the available sample information assumes the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rkhs-model-linear-2}
  Y_i \mid X_i, \theta \ \stackrel{\text{ind.}}{\sim} \mathcal N\left(\alpha + \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j X_i(t_j),\, \sigma^2\right), \quad i =1,\dots, n.
\end{equation}

It is worth mentioning that the model remains linear in the sense that it fundamentally involves a random variable \(\dotprod{X}{\beta}_K = \Psi_X(\beta)\) in the linear span of the process \(X\), thanks to Loève's isometry. Moreover, note that this \acs{RKHS} model is particularly suited as a basis for variable selection methods \citep[see][]{berrendero2019rkhs}.

\subsubsection*{Prior distribution}

A simple and intuitive prior distribution \(\Pi\) for the parameter vector \(\theta \in \Theta\), suggested by the structure of the parameter space and usually employed in the literature, is given by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prior-linear}
  \begin{array}{r@{\ }c@{\ }l@{\quad}l}
    p                    & \sim               & \pi,                   &                \\[0.2em]
    t_j                  & \textoversim{ind.} & \mathcal{U}[0,1],      & j = 1,\dots,p, \\[0.2em]
    \beta_j              & \textoversim{ind.} & \mathcal{N}(0,\eta^2), & j = 1,\dots,p, \\[0.2em]
    \Pi(\alpha,\sigma^2) & \propto            & \dfrac{1}{\sigma^2},   &
  \end{array}
\end{equation}
where  \(\pi\) is any discrete distribution on \(\N\) (e.g.~uniform on a given finite subset) and \(\eta^2 \in \R^+\) is a hyperparameter of the model that depends strongly on the expected scale of the data. On the one hand, note the use of a joint prior distribution on \(\alpha\) and \(\sigma^2\), which is a widely used noninformative prior known as Jeffrey's prior \citep{jeffreys1946invariant}. One should be wary of the Lindley's paradox when assigning improper priors \citep[see][]{robert2014jeffreys}, but since the two parameters involved are common to all possible values of \(p\), this formulation should not present difficulties. On the other hand, the prior on the coefficients \(\beta_j\) (which are interchangeable when coupled with their respective \(t_j\)) is deliberately kept simple and independent of \(p\), as this will greatly speed up and simplify the posterior computations later on. Nevertheless, the methods presented hereafter could be adapted to more complicated and dependent priors without too much trouble.

\subsubsection*{Posterior distribution}

Even though we cannot compute the posterior distribution in closed form, we can derive a function proportional to it. A standard algebraic manipulation using Bayes's formula yields the following result.

\begin{proposition}
  Under the linear \acs{RKHS} model~\eqref{eq:rkhs-model-linear-2} and the prior distribution~\eqref{eq:prior-linear}, the log-posterior distribution up to an additive constant is given by
  \begin{align*}
    \log\Pi_n(\theta\mid \mathcal D_n) \propto & -\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\|b_p\|^2}{\eta^2} + \frac{\|Y_{1:n} - \alpha 1_n - \mathcal X_{\tau_p} b_p\|^2}{\sigma^2} \right)\\
    &- (n+2)\log \sigma - p\log \eta\\
    &- \frac{p}{2}\log(2\pi) + \log \Pi(p),
  \end{align*}
  where \(Y_{1:n}=(Y_1,\dots,Y_n)'\), \(1_n\) is an \(n\)-dimensional vector of ones, and \(\mathcal X_{\tau_p}\) is the data matrix \(\{X_i(t_j): i=1,\dots,n,\, j=1,\dots,p\}\).
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Functional logistic regression}\label{sec:rkhs-logistic-model}

In the case of functional logistic regression, we regard the binary response variable \(Y\in\{0, 1\}\) as a Bernoulli random variable given the functional regressor \(X\). Then, following the approach suggested by \citet{berrendero2023functional}, a simplified logistic \acs{RKHS} model is given by the equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rkhs-model-logistic}
  \pp(Y=1 \mid X) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left\{-\alpha - \dotprod{X}{\beta}_K\right\}}, \quad \alpha \in \R, \ \beta \in \Hcal_{0}(K).
\end{equation}

Indeed, note that this can be seen as a finite-dimensional approximation (with a functional interpretation) to the general \acs{RKHS} functional logistic model proposed by these authors, which can be obtained by replacing \(\Hcal_{0}(K)\) with \(\Hcal(K)\). After incorporating the sample information, we can rewrite~\eqref{eq:rkhs-model-logistic} in parametric form for \(\theta\in\Theta\) as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rkhs-model-logistic-2}
  Y_i \mid X_i,\theta \ \stackrel{\text{ind.}}{\sim} \operatorname{Bernoulli}\left(\pp\left(Y_i=1 \mid X_i,\theta\right)\right), \quad i=1,\dots, n,
\end{equation}
where
\[
  \pp(Y_i=1 \mid X_i,\theta) = \frac{1}{\displaystyle 1 + \exp\left\{-\alpha - \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j X_i(t_j)\right\}}, \quad i=1,\dots, n.
\]

In much the same way as the linear regression model described above, this \acs{RKHS}-based logistic regression model offers some advantages over the classical \(L^2\)-model. First, it has a more straightforward interpretation and allows for a workable Bayesian approach. Secondly, it can be shown that, under mild conditions, the general \acs{RKHS} logistic functional model holds whenever the conditional distributions \(X | Y=i\) (\(i=0,1\)) are homoscedastic Gaussian processes, and in some cases it also entails the \(L^2\)-model~\citep[see][]{berrendero2023functional}; this arguably provides a solid theoretical motivation for the reduced model. Incidentally, these models also shed light on the near-perfect classification phenomenon for functional data, described by \citet{delaigle2012achieving} and further examined for example in the works of \citet{berrendero2018use} or \citet{torrecilla2020optimal}.

Furthermore, a maximum likelihood approach for parameter estimation (although not considered here) is possible as well, since the finite-dimensional approximation  mitigates the problem of nonexistence of the \acs{MLE} in the functional case. However, let us recall that even in finite-dimensional settings there are cases of quasi-complete separation in which the \acs{MLE} does not exist \citep{albert1984existence}. Additionally, the nonexistence issue of the \acs{MLE} becomes more pronounced as the dimension increases, as exemplified in the theory recently developed by \citet{candes2020phase}. In any event, we argue that the simplified \acs{RKHS} model presented here is a compelling and feasible approach to functional logistic regression, since it bypasses the main difficulties of the usual maximum likelihood techniques.

\subsubsection*{Prior distribution}

As far as prior distributions go, we proceed as we did in the linear model. However, following the advice in \citet{gelman2008weakly} and \citet{ghosh2018use}, we do change the prior of the coefficients \(\beta_j\) and the constant term \(\alpha\), since their interpretation is different now:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prior-logistic}
  \begin{array}{r@{\ }c@{\ }l@{\quad}l}
    p       & \sim               & \pi,                   &                \\[0.2em]
    t_j     & \textoversim{ind.} & \mathcal{U}[0,1],      & j = 1,\dots,p, \\[0.2em]
    \beta_j & \textoversim{ind.} & t_5(0,2.5),            & j = 1,\dots,p, \\[0.2em]
    \alpha  & \sim               & \text{Cauchy}(0,10). &
  \end{array}
  \end{equation}
The scaled Student's \(t\) and Cauchy distributions represent robust and weakly informative priors that provide shrinkage and can handle the case of separation in logistic regression. The specific values of the parameters have been chosen via experimentation following the initial recommendations in \citet{ghosh2018use}, which also establishes the need to scale the regressors to have mean zero and standard deviation \(0.5\). Note that in this case the nuisance parameter \(\sigma^2\) does not appear in the model.

\subsubsection*{Posterior distribution}

The posterior distribution in the logistic case is again derived via Bayes' formula.

\begin{proposition}
  Under the logistic \acs{RKHS} model~\eqref{eq:rkhs-model-logistic-2} and the prior distribution~\eqref{eq:prior-logistic}, the log-posterior distribution up to an additive constant is
  \begin{align*}
    \log \Pi_n(\theta \mid \mathcal D_n) \propto & \sum_{i=1}^n \biggl[Y_i \left(\alpha + \dotprod{X_i}{\beta}_K\right)\\
    & - \log\left(1 + \exp\left\{\alpha +\dotprod{X_i}{\beta}_K\right\}\right) \biggr]\\
    & - 3\sum_{j=1}^p \log(4\beta_j^2 + 125)\\
    & + p \left[\frac{15}{2}\log 5 + 4 \log 2 - \log (3\pi)\right]\\
    & - \log(100 + \alpha^2) + \log \Pi(p),
  \end{align*}
  where \(\beta(\cdot)=\sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j K(t_j, \cdot)\) and \(\dotprod{X_i}{\beta}_K = \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j X_i(t_j)\).
\end{proposition}

\section{Prediction strategies}\label{sec:prediction-bfr}

For the inference step in our Bayesian procedure, the usual approach would be to consider a fixed number of components, chosen separately via some model selection criterion, and then approximate the corresponding posterior distribution through computational methods. However, we aim to construct a fully Bayesian methodology that allows us to model the variable number of components and the component parameters jointly. 

Since we do not impose conjugate priors and the posterior distribution does not have a recognizable shape, a straightforward way to achieve the desired outcome is to use \acs{RJMCMC} to approximate the posterior distribution. There are several alternatives, such as product space formulations \citep{carlin1995bayesian} or Bayesian averaging/model selection methods \citep{hoeting1999bayesian}, but this is precisely the problem that \acs{RJMCMC} was designed to solve. The use of these samplers fits naturally within our framework, as they allow the complexity of the model to be directly chosen by the data, jointly inferring about the dimensionality and the value of the parameters.

From a higher level perspective, the \acs{RJMCMC} algorithm is an iterative procedure that produces a chain of \(R\) approximate samples 
\[
\theta^{(r)}=\left(p^{(r)}, \tau^{(r)}, b^{(r)}, \alpha^{(r)}, (\sigma^{2})^{(r)}\right), \quad r=1,\dots,R
\] 
of the posterior distribution \(\Pi_n(\theta| \mathcal D_n)\), each possibly of a different dimension (see Chapter~\ref{ch:rjmcmc} for a more detailed description). Given a previously unseen regressor \(X_{\text{test}}\), with each of these samples we can generate an individual approximate response following our \acs{RKHS} models, denoted by \(F(X_{\text{test}}, \theta^{(r)})\). In the linear case we sample from the distribution \(Y | X_{\text{test}}, \theta^{(r)}\) given in~\eqref{eq:rkhs-model-linear-2}, and in the logistic case we directly consider the probabilities \(\pp(Y=1|X_{\text{test}},\theta^{(r)})\) in~\eqref{eq:rkhs-model-logistic-2}. We propose to combine these predictions in four different ways.

\subsection{One-stage methods}

In these methods we essentially make use of the so-called posterior predictive distribution, and they are in turn divided in two approaches.

  \paragraph{Weighted sum (W-PP)} The first idea involves utilizing all available information by summarizing and aggregating every individual \acs{RKHS} response. We start by considering a point summary statistic \(g\) (such as the mean, median or mode) and consolidating the predictions from all sub-models, each having distinct dimension. Then, we bring together these predictions through a weighted sum, in which the weights correspond to the relative frequencies of the corresponding values of \(p\) (their approximate posterior):
  \[
  \hat Y = \sum_{p} \hat\Pi_n(p\mid\mathcal D_n) g\left(\{F(X_{\text{test}}, \theta^{(r)})\}_{r:p^{(r)}=p}\right),
  \]
  where \(\hat \Pi_n(p|\mathcal D_n) = R^{-1}\sum_r \mathds 1\{p^{(r)}=p\}\). In the logistic case, we employ the usual thresholding procedure to convert the final probability to a binary class label in \(\{0,1\}\). Note that this approach is reminiscent of posterior factorization \(\Pi_n(\theta|\mathcal D_n) = \sum_{p}\Pi_n(p|\mathcal D_n)\Pi_n(\tau,b,\alpha,\sigma^2|p, \mathcal D_n)\). We shall see in Section~\ref{sec:results-bfr} that this method produces the best results in practice.

  \paragraph{Maximum a posteriori (MAP-PP)} We also consider a \acf{MAP} strategy, where only the most probable sub-model is used and the rest of the samples are discarded. In this case, denoting \(\hat p = \arg\max_p \hat\Pi_n(p|\mathcal D_n)\), predictions are computed as 
  \[
  \hat Y = g\left(\{F(X_{\text{test}}, \theta^{(r)})\}_{r:p^{(r)}=\hat p}\right).
  \] 
  Although this method may ignore many samples in the posterior approximation, this omission can help reduce noise and prevent outliers from affecting the final prediction.

\subsection{Two-stage methods}

In these methods we focus only on the marginal posterior distribution of the impact points \(\tau_p = (t_1, \dots, t_p)\), disregarding the rest of the parameters and effectively constructing a variable selection procedure. After choosing a set of time instants \(\hat \tau_p=(\hat t_1,\dots, \hat t_p)\) using the approximate posterior samples \(\{\tau^{(r)}\}\), we can reduce the original functional regressors to just the marginals \(\{X_i(\hat \tau_p)\}=\{(X_i(\hat t_1),\dots, X_i(\hat t_p))\}\), and apply any of the well-known finite-dimensional prediction algorithms suited for this situation.

\paragraph{Weighted sum (W-VS)} We can mirror the weighted sum approach of the one-stage methods, with predictions computed as
\[
\hat Y = \sum_{p} \hat\Pi_n(p\mid \mathcal D_n) G(X_{\text{test}}, \hat \tau_{p}),
\]
where \(\hat \tau_{p}=(\hat t_1, \dots ,\hat t_p)=g(\{\tau^{(r)}\}_{r:p^{(r)}=p})\) and \(g\) is a component-wise summary statistic. The function \(G\) represents the prediction for \(X_{\text{test}}\) of a regular linear/logistic regression algorithm, fitted with the transformed data set \(\{(X_i(\hat \tau_p), Y_i): i=1,\dots,n\}\).

\paragraph{Maximum a posteriori (MAP-VS)} We also consider a \acs{MAP} approach to variable selection with only the information of the most probable sub-model, i.e., 
\[
\hat Y = G(X_{\text{test}}, \hat \tau_{\hat p}).
\]

\section{Posterior consistency}\label{sec:consistency-bfr}

This section explores the theoretical foundations of our Bayesian models in the context of predictive inference. In particular, we establish results grounded in the theory of posterior consistency introduced in Section~\ref{sec:posterior-consistency-intro}, which provide rigorous justification for the reliability of these functional models in asymptotic settings (from a frequentist perspective). We focus on the linear case, but the results we obtain generally hold \textit{mutatis mutandis} in the logistic case. All technical details and proofs are deferred to Section~\ref{sec:proofs-bfr}.

Recall that in our functional linear model the data is an i.i.d.\ sample \((X_1, Y_1), \ldots, (X_n, Y_n)\) of a random vector \((X,Y)\), where \(X=X(t)\) is a second-order stochastic process taking values in \(\mathcal X=\mathcal C[0,1]\), and \(Y\) is a random variable taking values in \(\mathcal Y=\R\). The parameter space is the disjoint union \(\Theta=\bigcup_p \Theta_p\), defined in~\eqref{eq:theta-parameter-space-bfr}, so that each \(\theta\in\Theta\) belongs to exactly one of the \(\Theta_p\). As usual, we equip both \(\mathcal X \times \mathcal Y\) and \(\Theta\) with their respective Borel sigma-algebras, and observe that both are Polish spaces.

Let \(\pp_{\theta}(X,Y)\) be the joint data distribution in terms of \(\theta\in\Theta\), and note that formally it factorizes as \(\pp_{\theta}(X,Y)\equiv\qq_X \pp_\theta(Y|X)\). Here \(\qq_X\) is the distribution of the process \(X\) on path space, and in our \acs{RKHS} setting, \(\pp_\theta(Y|X)\) is the normal distribution \(\mathcal N(\alpha + \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j X(t_j),\, \sigma^2)\). Moreover, for convenience, define \((X,Y)_{1:\infty} := (X_1, Y_1), (X_2, Y_2), \dots\) and \((X,Y)_{1:n} := (X_1,Y_1), (X_2, Y_2), \dots, (X_n, Y_n)\). The full hierarchical model under consideration is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:model-linear}
  \begin{split}
    \text{(no.\ of components)}\quad & p \sim \pi,                                                                                    \\
    \text{(component times)}\quad    & \tau \mid p \sim \mathfrak{T}_p,                                                                     \\
    \text{(impact points)}\quad   & b \mid p \sim \mathfrak{B}_p,                                                                        \\
    \text{(intercept)}\quad          & \alpha \sim \Gamma,                                                                                     \\
    \text{(error variance)}\quad     & \sigma^2 \sim \Delta,                                                                                   \\
    \text{(observed data)}\quad      & (X,Y)_{1:n} \mid \theta \sim \pp_{\theta}(X,Y) \quad \text{i.i.d.},
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
where \(\theta = (p, \tau, b, \alpha, \sigma^2)\) is a random variable taking values in \(\Theta\), and \(\pi\), \(\mathfrak{T}_p\), \(\mathfrak{B}_p\), \(\Gamma\) and \(\Delta\) are probability measures on \(\N\), \([0,1]^p\), \(\R^p\), \(\R\) and \(\R^+_0\), respectively. We denote by \(\Pi\) the prior distribution on \(\theta\) implied by the model in~\eqref{eq:model-linear}. Further, we suppose that the model is well-specified, so that there exists \(\theta_0\in\Theta_{p_0}\) such that \((X,Y)\sim \pp_{\theta_0}(X,Y)\), and denote by \(\pp_0^\infty(X,Y)\) and \(\pp_0^{(n)}(X,Y)\) the probability measures of \((X,Y)_{1:\infty}\) and \((X,Y)_{1:n}\), respectively, when \(\theta_0\) is the true value of the parameter.

\subsection{Consistency via Doob's theorem}

At this point we can follow an approach very similar to the one in \citet{miller2023consistency}, where posterior consistency is established through Doob's theorem (Theorem~\ref{th:doob}) in a mixture model with an unknown number of components, which has an infinite-dimensional parameter space that factorizes in the same way as~\eqref{eq:theta-parameter-space-bfr}. Considering that we will only be interested in small balls around the true value of the parameter, we can define a bounded metric for \(\theta_p, \tilde\theta_{\tilde p} \in \Theta\) by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:metric-doob}
  d_\Theta(\theta_p, \tilde\theta_{\tilde p})= \begin{cases}
    \min \left\{\|\theta_p - \tilde\theta_{\tilde p}\|,\, 1\right\}, \quad & \text{if } p=\tilde{p}, \\
    1, \quad                                           & \text{otherwise}.
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Since each \(\Theta_p\) is itself a complete separable metric space with the inherited Euclidean norm, Proposition A.1 in \citet{miller2023consistency} ensures that \((\Theta, d_\Theta)\) is a complete separable metric space. Note that \(\pp_\theta(X,Y)\) is invariant under permutations of the component labels \(\tau\) and \(b\), so in an identifiable model we can only show consistency in the parameter space up to one such permutation. To that effect, let \(\mathfrak{S}_p\) denote the set of permutations of \(\{1,\dots,p\}\), and for \(\theta \in \Theta_p\) and any \(\nu\in \mathfrak{S}_p\), denote by \(\theta[\nu]\) the result of applying the permutation \(\nu\) to the component labels of \(\theta\). That is, if \(\theta=(p, t_1,\dots, t_p, \beta_1,\dots,\beta_p, \alpha,\sigma^2)\), then \(\theta[\nu]=(p, t_{\nu_1},\dots, t_{\nu_p}, \beta_{\nu_1},\dots,\beta_{\nu_p}, \alpha,\sigma^2)\). Next, for a given \(\theta_0\in\Theta_{p_0}\) and \(\epsilon>0\), denote \(\mathfrak{S}_0=\mathfrak{S}_{p_0}\) and define the permutation-invariant neighborhood 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:neighborhood-doob-permutation}
  B_{d_\Theta}^{\mathfrak{S}}(\theta_0, \epsilon) =\bigcup_{\nu\in \mathfrak{S}_0} \left\{\theta \in \Theta: d_\Theta(\theta_0[\nu], \theta) < \epsilon \right\},
\end{equation}
which consists of all parameters that are within \(\epsilon\) (in the metric \(d_\Theta\)) of some relabeling of \(\theta_0\). Now, for identifiability to hold, we need to place some very mild restrictions on the prior distribution.
\begin{assumption} Under the model in~\eqref{eq:model-linear}, for all \(p\in\N\):\label{cond:condition-ident}
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item \(\Pi(t_i=t_j\mid p)=0\) for all \(1\leq i < j \leq p\).
    \item \(\Pi(\beta_j=0 \mid p)=0\) for all \(1\leq j \leq p\).
  \end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}
Both conditions are automatically satisfied if one chooses prior distributions which have a density with respect to Lebesgue measure. They can be interpreted as a way of pursuing parsimony in the model, aiming for as few components as possible. With this setup in mind, we are ready to state our consistency result.

\begin{theorem}\label{th:consistency-doob-linear}
  \marginproof{proof:consistency-doob-linear}
  Suppose that~\ref{cond:condition-ident} holds. Then, there exists \(\Theta_*\subseteq \Theta\) such that \(\Pi(\theta \in \Theta_*)=1\) and for all \(\theta_0\in\Theta_*\), if \((X,Y)_{1:\infty} \sim \pp_{\theta_0}(X,Y)\) i.i.d., then for any \(\epsilon > 0\)
  \[
    \lim_{n\to\infty} \Pi_n\left(\theta \in B_{d_\Theta}^{\mathfrak{S}}(\theta_0, \epsilon) \mathrel{\big\vert} (X,Y)_{1:n}\right) = 1 \quad \pp_{0}^\infty(X,Y)-\text{a.s.}
  \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
  In particular, by taking \(0<\epsilon<1\) we immediately obtain  
  \[
    \lim_{n\to\infty} \Pi_n\left(p=p_0 \mid (X,Y)_{1:n}\right) = 1 \quad \pp_{0}^\infty(X,Y)-\text{a.s.}
  \]
  This statement is of certain relevance in itself, because the estimation of the number of components in finite mixture models is a hard problem in general \citep[see][and references therein]{miller2018mixture, cai2021finite}.
\end{remark}

It is worth pointing out that the proofs of the results above can be easily adjusted to guarantee consistency when the number of components \(p\) is fixed beforehand and the parameter space is finite-dimensional, as long as the model remains well-specified.

\subsubsection*{A remark on Lebesgue consistency}

All in all, Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-doob-linear} guarantees consistency for \(\Pi\)-almost every parameter in the support of the prior distribution. However, even though we can choose the prior so that \(\supp( \Pi) = \Theta\), in principle there is no assurance that the \(\Pi\)-null set in which consistency may fail will not be a large set with respect to other measures. In fact, when the parameter space is infinite-dimensional there are examples of big inconsistency sets, even for reasonably chosen prior distributions~\citep{diaconis1986consistency}. Nonetheless, this problem can be alleviated when the parameter space is a countable union of disjoint finite-dimensional sets. First, note that there is a natural extension of the Lebesgue measure to our parameter space \(\Theta\): just consider the genuine Lebesgue measure \(\mathfrak{m}_{2p+2}\) on the continuous coordinates of \(\Theta_p\), and for all \(B\subseteq \Theta\) measurable define 
\[
\mathfrak{m}_\infty(B) := \sum_{p=1}^\infty \mathfrak{m}_{2p+2}(\Theta_p \cap B).
\]
Then, if we choose a prior distribution with respect to which this measure is absolutely continuous, the exceptional set in Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-doob-linear} will satisfy \(\mathfrak{m}_\infty(\Theta \setminus \Theta_*)=0\) and thus be ``small'' with respect to a Lebesgue-type measure. In our case, this requirement can be weakened because consistency is invariant under permutations of the component labels: it suffices that every set with positive Lebesgue measure have positive prior probability for at least one permutation of the component labels.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:consistency-lebesgue-linear}
  \marginproof{proof:consistency-lebesgue-linear}
  Suppose that~\ref{cond:condition-ident} holds. Furthermore, assume that for all \(p\in\N\) and all \(B_p\subseteq \Theta_p\) measurable
  \[
  \sum_{\nu\in \mathfrak{S}_p} \Pi(\theta[\nu] \in B_p) = 0 \Longrightarrow \mathfrak{m}_{2p+2}(B_p)=0.
  \]
  Then the conclusion of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-doob-linear} remains valid with \(\mathfrak{m}_\infty(\Theta \setminus \Theta_*)=0\).
\end{proposition}

The condition is met, for example, if \(\Pi(p)>0\) and \((\tau, b, \alpha, \sigma^2)| p\) has a density with respect to Lebesgue measure that is positive almost everywhere, for all \(p\in\N\). A similar approach is considered in \citet{nobile1994bayesian} and \citet{miller2023consistency} to establish ``Lebesgue''-almost sure consistency in finite mixture models with a prior on the number of components.

\subsection{Consistency and contraction rates via Schwartz's theorem}

The Doob-type results of the previous section, although interesting, are still assertions on consistency from the point of view of the prior distribution. We now seek additional results that offer more definitive statements and, more importantly, establish posterior contraction rates. To this end, we consider the conditional model \(Y|X=x, \theta \sim f_\theta(y|x)\), where \(\theta\in\Theta\) is a parameter vector and \(f_\theta(y|x)\) is the density of \(Y\) given \(X=x\) with respect to the Lebesgue measure \(\mathfrak{m}\) on \(\mathcal Y=\R\). Under our assumptions, \(f_\theta(y|x)\) is the density of the normal distribution given by~\eqref{eq:rkhs-model-linear-2}, as long as \(\sigma^2>0\). We could work with this model and analyze the fixed design case with data that is not i.i.d.\ \citep[see][]{choi2008remarks}, but we choose to focus on the arguably more significant random design case.

In general, in infinite-dimensional models there is no reference measure with respect to which to define a density, and to apply the consistency results derived from Schwartz's theorem we need a dominated model (see Section~\ref{sec:posterior-consistency-intro}). As it turns out, in our specific functional regression setting we can find such a density through the following observation. 

\begin{lemma}
  \phantomsection\label{lemma:joint-density-rho} % force correct anchor
  \marginproof{proof:joint-density-rho}
  The function \((x,y)\mapsto f_\theta(y|x)\) is a joint probability density of \((X,Y)\) with respect to the product measure \(\rho=\qq_X\times \mathfrak{m}\), where \(\qq_X\) is the law of the process \(X\).
\end{lemma}

In this way, denoting \(f_\theta := f_\theta(y|x)\) and \(\mathcal F=\{f_\theta: \theta \in \Theta\}\), we can express our full model (with respect to the measure \(\rho\)) as
\begin{align*}
  (X, Y)_{1:n} \mid f_\theta &\stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} f_\theta,\\
  f_\theta &\sim \Pi_{\mathcal F},
\end{align*}
where \(\Pi_{\mathcal F}\) is a prior distribution on the parameter class \(\mathcal F\). In practice, we work with the prior distribution \(\Pi\) on \(\Theta\) specified in~\eqref{eq:model-linear}, and interpret \(\Pi_{\mathcal F}\) as the pushforward measure of \(\Pi\) by the measurable mapping \(\theta \mapsto f_\theta\). As always, we assume the existence of a ``true'' density function \(f_0 :=f_{\theta_0}(y|x)\in \mathcal F\) that generates the observations. Moreover, we denote \(\Theta_0=\Theta_{p_0}\).

The idea now is to apply Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-hellinger} to obtain posterior consistency with respect to the Hellinger distance \(d_H\). Looking at the form of the parameter space \(\Theta\) in~\eqref{eq:theta-parameter-space-bfr}, a natural choice for the sieves is 
\[
\Theta_n = \bigcup_{p=1}^{p_n} \Theta_p\quad \text{and}\quad  \mathcal F_n=\left\{f_\theta\in\mathcal F: \theta \in \Theta_n\right\},
\]
for \(n\geq 1\), where \(p_n=O(h(n))\) and \(h(n)\to \infty\) is a function to be determined later. Moreover, to keep the conditions as simple as possible, it is more convenient to work with compact parameter spaces \(\Theta_p\) and adapt the prior distributions in~\eqref{eq:model-linear} accordingly; we assume this is so for the remainder of the section. 

We are ready to state a new Hellinger consistency result for our model, which transforms the requirement that \(f_0\in \kl(\Pi_{\mathcal F})\) into a condition on the prior \(\Pi\) on \(\Theta_0\), and places an additional mild constraint on the tails of the marginal prior distribution of \(p\). These conditions guarantee that we can choose a suitable growth order for \(p_n\) in the sieve to apply Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-hellinger}. In order to satisfy all the conditions, we also need to impose some smoothness restrictions on the underlying process \(X\).

\begin{assumption}\label{cond:condition-schwartz-X}
  Suppose that \(X\) is \textit{mean-square Lipschitz continuous}, i.e., there exists \(L>0\) such that \(\E[(X(t) - X(s))^2] \leq L (t-s)^2\) for all \(t, s \in [0,1]\).
\end{assumption}

\begin{theorem}\label{th:consistency-schwartz-linear}
  \marginproof{proof:consistency-schwartz-linear}
  Assume~\ref{cond:condition-schwartz-X} holds, and suppose that there exist \(\delta > 0\) and \(k>1\) such that \(\Pi(p)=O(e^{-\delta p (\log p)^k})\) as \(p\to\infty\). If \(\Pi\) assigns positive mass to every open neighborhood of \(\theta_0\) in \(\Theta_0\), then the posterior distribution in the model \(\theta \sim \Pi\) and \((X,Y)_{1:n}|\theta \sim f_\theta\) i.i.d.\ is consistent relative to \(d_H\) at \(f_0\), i.e., for every \(\epsilon>0\) it holds that
  \[
    \lim_{n\to\infty} \Pi_n\left(\theta: d_H(f_0, f_\theta) < \epsilon \mid (X,Y)_{1:n}\right)=1\quad \pp_0^\infty(X,Y)-\text{a.s.}
  \]
\end{theorem}

An immediate observation is that if \(\Pi(p)\) is supported on a finite set, then the prior tail condition is automatically verified. Moreover, since this condition only needs to hold for large values of \(p\), we can always consider a hybrid prior distribution that changes at some large cutoff point to have rapidly decaying tails.

Following a similar proof strategy as in Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-schwartz-linear}, we can find a convenient sieve to apply Theorem~\ref{th:contraction-rate} and obtain posterior contraction rates in the Hellinger distance.

\begin{theorem}\label{th:contraction-rate-schwartz-linear}
  \marginproof{proof:contraction-rate-schwartz-linear}
Assume~\ref{cond:condition-schwartz-X} holds. Let \(0<\gamma<1/2\) and suppose that \(\Pi(p)=O(e^{-\delta p \log p})\) as \(p\to\infty\), for some \(\delta>32(1-\gamma)/(1-2\gamma)\). If \(\Pi\) has a density on the continuous components of \(\Theta_0\) with respect to Lebesgue measure that is bounded away from zero in a neighborhood of \(\theta_0\), then the posterior distribution in the model \(\theta \sim \Pi\) and \((X,Y)_{1:n}|\theta \sim f_\theta\) i.i.d.\ contracts at a rate \(\epsilon_n= n^{-\gamma}\) relative to \(d_H\) at \(f_0\), i.e., for every sequence \(M_n\to\infty\) it holds that
\[
    \Pi_n\left(\theta: d_H(f_0, f_\theta) \geq M_n \epsilon_n \mid (X,Y)_{1:n}\right) \to 0 \quad \text{in } \pp_0^{(n)}(X,Y)\text{-probability}.
    \]
\end{theorem}

The requirements of this result are akin to those of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-schwartz-linear}, but the conclusion is considerably stronger. To verify the condition on the density of \(\Pi\), provided that it exists, it suffices to show that it is continuous and positive at the continuous components of \(\theta_0\) (which our proposed prior distributions satisfy). The contraction rate \(\epsilon_n=n^{-\gamma}\) with \(0<\gamma<1/2\) is optimal in the sense that it is as close as desired to the parametric rate of \(n^{-1/2}\), and it is \textit{minimax optimal} for estimators of \(f_\theta\) given the class \(\mathcal F_n\) \citep[see][p.~198 and references therein]{ghosal2017fundamentals}.

It is worth pointing out that a contraction rate relative to \(d_H\) is also a contraction rate relative to any distance bounded above by a multiple of the Hellinger distance, so we immediately have consistency and a contraction rate in more descriptive distances. Let \([h]_M\) represent the real-valued function \(h\) truncated to the interval \([-M, M]\), and denote the \(L^2(\qq_X)\)-norm by \(\|\cdot\|_{2,\qq_X}\).

\begin{corollary}\label{corollary:contraction-rate-schwartz-linear-2}
  \marginproof{proof:contraction-rate-schwartz-linear-2}
  Under the same conditions as in Theorem~\ref{th:contraction-rate-schwartz-linear}, the posterior distribution contracts at a rate \(\epsilon_n=n^{-\gamma}\) \((0<\gamma<1/2)\) at \(f_0\), relative to the \(L^1\)-distance 
  \[
    d_1(f_0, f_\theta)=\int |f_0 - f_\theta|\, d\rho
  \] 
  and to the mean-variance discrepancy metric 
  \[
    d_{2,\qq_X}(\theta_0, \theta) = \|[\mu_{\theta_0}]_M - [\mu_\theta]_M\|_{2,\qq_X} + |\sigma_0^2 - \sigma^2|,
  \]
  for any \(M>0\), where \(\mu_\theta(x)= \alpha + \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j x(t_j)\).
\end{corollary}

To conclude, observe that these results are valid for the functional \acs{RKHS} linear model. In the case of the functional logistic model, the reference measure would be \(\rho=\qq_X \times \#\), where \(\#\) is the counting measure on \(\{0,1\}\). We would then work with the conditional density \(f_\theta(y|x)=H_\theta(x)^y (1-H_\theta(x))^{1-y}\), where \(H_\theta(x)=\pp(Y=1|x,\theta)\) is the parameter of the Bernoulli distribution in the logistic model~\eqref{eq:rkhs-model-logistic-2}. At this point, we could follow a similar strategy as in the linear case and obtain analogous results; see Section 6 in \citet{ghosal2006posterior} and the proof of Theorem~7.20 in \citet{ghosal2017fundamentals}.

\subsubsection*{On the smoothness requirements}

Note that~\ref{cond:condition-schwartz-X} guarantees, via Kolmogorov's continuity theorem, that the sample paths of \(X\) (after an eventual modification) are continuous functions. Apart from somewhat trivial examples, such as a deterministic process or a linear random process, we can show that processes with a sufficiently smooth covariance satisfy our requirements.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:mean-square-lipschitz}
  \marginproof{proof:mean-square-lipschitz}
  If the covariance function \(K\) of the process \(X\) is twice-continuously differentiable and \(\E[X(t)]=0\) for all \(t\in[0,1]\), then \(X\) is mean-square Lipschitz continuous.
\end{lemma}

Note that the above requirement is by no means a necessary one. For example, given a second-order stochastic process \(\{W(t): t \in [0,1]\}\) with zero mean, we can consider the integrated process
\[
  X(t)=\int_0^t W(u)\, du, \quad t\in [0,1].
\]
If \(K_W(t,s)=\E[W(t)W(s)]\) is uniformly bounded above by a constant \(L>0\) on \([0,1]\), we have
\[
  \E\left[(X(t) - X(s))^2\right] = \int_s^t \int_s^t \E[W(u)W(v)]\, du\,dv \leq L(t-s)^2.
\]
For example, we might take \(W(t)\) to be a standard Brownian motion, an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process or, in general, any second-order process with a continuous covariance function. In any case, by virtue of Lemma~\ref{lemma:mean-square-lipschitz} we can always pre-process the trajectories so that they are regular enough for the conditions to hold, considering for instance the convolution with a smooth kernel.


\section{Experimental results and analysis}\label{sec:results-bfr}

In this section we present the results of the experiments carried out to test the performance of our functional regression methods in different scenarios.\footnote{The Python code is available at \url{https://github.com/antcc/rk-bfr-jump}.} We consider both simulated and real data, divided into training and testing sets with a 66\%/33\% split. The models are fitted on the training data, using \acs{RJMCMC} to sample from the approximate posterior, and then out-of-sample predictions are computed on the testing set. We independently repeat the whole process 10 times (each with a different train/test configuration) to account for the stochasticity in the sampling step, and average the results across these executions. For the purposes of prediction we consider the point statistics \textit{trimmed mean} (\(10\%\)), \textit{median} and \textit{mode} to aggregate together predictions and summarize parameters (see Section~\ref{sec:prediction-bfr}). The initial experiments carried out indicate that low values of \(p\) provide sufficient flexibility in most scenarios, so we allow the number of components to vary in the set \(\{1,2,\dots,10\}\). Moreover, the values of \(\{t_j\}\) that fall outside the specified grid are truncated to the nearest neighbor in the grid, with the additional restriction that time instants in different components of our models cannot be equal.

The Python library used to perform the \acs{RJMCMC} approximation is \slsc{Eryn} \citep{karnesis2023eryn}. It is a toolbox for Bayesian inference that allows transdimensional posterior sampling, running multiple chains in an ensemble configuration. In the experiments we use 64 chains and run them for 5000 iterations in total, discarding the first 4000 as burn-in. Because of execution time constraints, the rest of the hyperparameters of the \slsc{Eryn} sampler are selected manually based on preliminary experiments and the authors' recommendations; this reduction in computational cost renders the sampling step practically viable. Moreover, some amount of post-processing is needed to mitigate the well-known label switching phenomenon that occurs in \acs{RJMCMC} approximations of finite mixture models. We give more details on the implementation in Chapter~\ref{ch:rjmcmc}.

\subsection{Data sets}

For the simulations we consider 200 training samples and 100 testing samples, with functional regressors measured on an equispaced grid of 100 points on \([0, 1]\). Furthermore, we choose a set of functional regressors common to linear and logistic regression problems. They are four zero-mean \acf{GPs}, each with a different covariance function:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A Brownian motion (\slsc{BM}), with kernel \(K_1(t,s)=\min\{t,s\}\) (excluding the origin to ensure strict positive definiteness).
  \item A fractional Brownian motion (\slsc{fBM}), with Hurst parameter \(H=0.8\) and kernel \(K_2(t,s)=1/2(s^{2H} + t^{2H} - |t-s|^{2H})\).
  \item An Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process (\slsc{OU}), with kernel \(K_3(t,s)=e^{-|t-s|}\).
  \item A \acs{GP} with a squared exponential kernel (\slsc{SE}), also known as Gaussian kernel, given by \(K_4(t,s)=e^{-(t-s)^2/2\xi^2}\) with \(\xi=0.2\). 
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Linear regression data sets} We employ three different types of simulated data sets, with a common value of \(\alpha=5\) for the intercept and \(\sigma^2=0.5\) for the error variance.
\begin{itemize}
  \item A finite-dimensional \acs{RKHS} response with three components for each of the four \acs{GP} regressors mentioned above, namely
  \[
    Y=5 -5X(0.1) + 5X(0.6) + 10X(0.8) + \epsilon.
  \]
  \item A ``componentless'' response generated by an \(L^2\)-model with a smooth underlying coefficient function, again for the same four \acs{GPs}:
  \[
    Y = 5 + \int_0^1 X(t)\log(1+4t)\, dt + \epsilon.
  \]
  \item A non-Gaussian regressor variable, specifically a geometric Brownian motion (\slsc{GBM}), given by \(X(t)=\exp\{\BM(t)\}\), where \(\BM(t)\) is a standard Brownian motion. We consider the same \acs{RKHS} and \(L^2\) response variables as above.
\end{itemize}
As for the real data experiments, we use the Tecator data set \citep{borggaard1992optimal} to predict fat content based on near-infrared absorbance curves of 193 meat samples, as well as what we call the Moisture \citep{kalivas1997two} and Sugar \citep{bro1999exploratory} data sets. The first consists of near-infrared spectra of 100 wheat samples and the objective is to predict the samples' moisture content, whereas the second contains 268 samples of sugar fluorescence data in order to predict ash content. The regressors of the three data sets are measured on a grid of 100, 101 and 115 equispaced points on \([0, 1]\), respectively.

\paragraph{Logistic regression data sets} Again we consider three different types of simulated data sets.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Four logistic finite-dimensional \acs{RKHS} responses with the same functional parameter as in the linear regression case (one for each \acs{GP}). Specifically,
  \[
    \pp(Y=1\mid X) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left\{0.5 +5X(0.1) - 5X(0.6) - 10X(0.8)\right\}}.
  \]
  \item Four logistic responses following an \(L^2\)-model with the same coefficient function as in the linear regression case, i.e., \(\beta(t)=\log(1+4t)\), and an intercept \(\alpha=-0.5\).
  \item A non-Gaussian mixture situation in which we combine regressors from two different \acs{GPs} with equal probability, and label them according to their origin. In particular, we consider a homoscedastic case to distinguish between a standard Brownian motion and a Brownian motion with mean function
  \[
  \E[X(t)] = \begin{cases}
    0,& \quad 0 \leq t < 0.5,\\
    0.75t,&\quad 0.5\leq t \leq 1;
  \end{cases}
  \]
  and a heteroscedastic case to distinguish between a standard Brownian motion and a Brownian motion with kernel \(K(t,s)=2\min\{t,s\}\).
\end{itemize}
Additionally, we use three real data sets well known in the literature. The first one is a subset of the Medflies data set \citep{carey1998relationship}, consisting on samples of the number of eggs laid daily by 534 flies over 30 days, to predict whether their longevity is high or low. The second one is the Berkeley Growth Study data set \citep{tuddenham1954physical}, which records the height of 54 girls and 39 boys over 31 different points in their lives. Finally, we select a subset of the Phoneme data set \citep{hastie1995penalized} based on 200 digitized speech frames over 128 equispaced points, to predict the phonemes ``aa'' and ``ao''.

\subsection{Comparison algorithms}

We have included a fairly comprehensive suite of comparison algorithms, chosen among the most common frequentist methods used in machine learning and \acs{FDA}, and following a standard implementation and choice of hyperparameters. There are purely functional methods, such as the usual \(L^2\) regression based on model~\eqref{eq:l2-linear-model}, finite-dimensional models that work on the discretized data (e.g.\ penalized finite-dimensional regression), and variable selection/dimension reduction procedures.

In the linear regression case, we have chosen the following algorithms:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Partial least squares regression (\slsc{PLS1}), as in \citet{abdi2010partial}.
  \item Linear least squares with \(l^1\) regularization (\slsc{Lasso}).
  \item Functional partial least squares regression through basis expansion (\slsc{FPLS} and \slsc{FPLS1}), implemented as in \citet{aguilera2010using}.
  \item Standard \(L^2\) functional linear regression model with fixed basis expansion and regularization (\slsc{FLin}).
  \item Functional partial least squares regression (\slsc{APLS}), proposed by \citet{delaigle2012methodology}.
  \item Partial least squares for dimension reduction (\slsc{PLS}).
  \item Principal component analysis for dimension reduction (\slsc{PCA}).
  \item Dummy variable selection method (\slsc{Manual}), with a pre-specified number of components equispaced on \([0, 1]\).
  \item Functional principal component analysis (\slsc{FPCA}).
  \item Linear least squares with \(l^2\) regularization (\slsc{Ridge}). This is used as the multiple regression that follows variable selection or dimensionality reduction methods.
\end{itemize}

In the logistic regression case, all the variable selection and dimension reduction techniques from above were also considered, with the addition of the following classification methods:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Maximum depth classifier (\slsc{MDC}), as in \citet{ghosh2005maximum}.
  \item Linear discriminant analysis (\slsc{LDA}).
  \item Functional nearest centroid classifier with the \(L^2\)-distance (\slsc{FNC}).
  \item Functional \acs{RKHS}-based logistic regression algorithm (\slsc{FLog}), proposed by \citet{berrendero2023functional}.
  \item Functional version of linear discriminant analysis (\slsc{FLDA}), proposed by \citet{preda2007pls}.
  \item Functional \(K\)-nearest neighbors classifier with the \(L^2\)-distance (\slsc{FKNN}).
  \item \acs{RKHS}-based variable selection and classification method (\slsc{RKVS}), proposed by \citet{berrendero2018use}.
  \item Functional partial least squares used as a dimension reduction method in combination with the nearest centroid algorithm (\slsc{APLS+NC}), as proposed by \citet{delaigle2012achieving}.
  \item Standard multiple logistic regression with \(l^2\) regularization (\slsc{Log}). This is used as a one-stage method and also as the multiple regression that follows variable selection or dimensionality reduction methods.
  \item Quadratic discriminant analysis (\slsc{QDA}).
\end{itemize}

The main hyperparameters of all these algorithms were selected by 10-fold cross-validation, and for those that have a number of components to select, we set 10 as the maximum value so that comparison with our own methods are fair. In particular, regularization parameters are searched among 20 values in the logarithmic space \([10^{-4}, 10^4]\), the number of basis elements for cubic spline bases is in \(\{4,5,\dots,10\}\), the number of basis elements for Fourier bases is one of \(\{1,3,5,7,9\}\), and the number of neighbors in the KNN classifier is in \(\{3,5,7,9,11,13\}\). Most algorithms have been taken from the Python libraries \slsc{scikit-learn} \citep{pedregosa2011scikit} and \slsc{scikit-fda} \citep{ramos2024scikit}, the first oriented to machine learning in general and the second to \acs{FDA} in particular. However, some methods were not found in these packages and had to be implemented from scratch. This is the case of the \slsc{FLDA}, \slsc{FPLS} and \slsc{APLS} methods, which we coded following the corresponding articles.

We have adopted a visual approach to presenting the experimentation results, using colored graphs to help visualize them. In each case, the mean and standard deviation of the score obtained across the 10 independent runs is shown, depicting our methods in \firstcolor{blue} and the comparison algorithms in \secondcolor{green}. We also show the global mean of all the comparison algorithms with a dashed vertical line, excluding extreme negative results to avoid distortion. \subsubsection*{Functional linear regression}

Following \citet{nobile2007bayesian}, we select a truncated Poisson prior with a low rate parameter \(\lambda=3\) for the number of components \(p\), so that simpler models are favored. However, in the experiments with real data we set \(p\sim \mathcal U\{1,2,\dots,10\}\) to allow a less informative exploration of the parameter space. Moreover, for simplicity we choose to scale the regressors and response to have standard deviation unity in the inference step, but then convert the results back to the original scale for prediction. This allows us to set a reasonable value of \(\eta^2=25\) in the weakly informative prior of \(\beta_j\) in~\eqref{eq:prior-linear}. The metric used to evaluate the performance is the \acf{RMSE}.

We begin with the simulation experiments in Figure~\ref{fig:reg_rkhs}, where we see the results for the \acs{RKHS} response. This is our baseline and the most favorable case for us, as the underlying model coincides with our assumed model. Indeed, we can see that in most instances our algorithms are the ones with lower \acs{RMSE}, as expected, and there is not much variance among our different approaches to prediction, at least in the one-stage methods. We even manage to consistently beat the \slsc{Lasso} finite-dimensional regression mechanism, which can select all 100 components for its model, versus our 10 components at most. This is an indication that our models make a more efficient selection of variables, encapsulating more information with fewer components and justifying our partiality to parsimonious models.

Figure~\ref{fig:reg_l2} shows the results for an underlying \(L^2\)-model, which would be our direct competitor and a more representative test for our Bayesian methods. In this case the outcome is satisfactory, as for the most part our models are on a par with the rest, even surpassing other methods that were designed with the \(L^2\)-model in mind. Especially interesting is the comparison with the standard \(L^2\) functional regression \slsc{FLin}, which we outperform most of the time.

The comparison results for non-Gaussian data can be seen in Figure~\ref{fig:reg_non_gp}. In this case our methods still get better results under the \acs{RKHS} model, while the results under the \(L^2\)-model are essentially the same, which is a positive outcome.

\begin{figure}[tbp]

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:fr-discussion}

We have introduced a natural and computationally feasible way of integrating Bayesian inference into functional regression models, by means of a \acs{RKHS} approach that simplifies the inherently hard task of setting a prior distribution on an infinite-dimensional space. The proposed \acs{RKHS} formulation gives a common framework to all finite-dimensional models based on linear combinations of the marginals of the underlying process, establishing a solid theoretical foundation for these popular points-of-impact models while retaining a functional viewpoint. Our approximation relies on simpler functional parameters, thus enhancing interpretability and ease of implementation. Moreover, it enables meaningful model comparisons across different dimensions, while also facilitating the analysis of the effects of local characteristics of the process.

We have also proved posterior consistency results that ensure the coherence and correctness of the Bayesian methods we developed. Similar conclusions in other settings often depend on substantially more restrictive conditions; here, introducing \acs{RKHSs} is key to significantly simplifying them. On the one hand, we have successfully adapted the methodology recently introduced in \citet{miller2023consistency}---originally intended for mixture models---to the fundamentally different scenario of functional regression, obtaining ``Lebesgue''-almost sure consistency for both the model parameters and the unknown number of components. On the other hand, we have derived alternative consistency results and optimal contraction rates through a more sophisticated approach based on Schwartz's theorem, which is the standard tool in nonparametric consistency problems. In addition, it is worth mentioning that the results we obtained are applicable to a wide range of prior distributions, including many that are rather simple and hence easier to work with.

Lastly, we have presented numerical evidence that supports the proposed Bayesian methodology and its predictive performance with simulated and real data sets. Thanks to our \acs{RKHS} formulation, we can effectively leverage the capabilities of \acs{RJMCMC} samplers and integrate the unknown number of components into the Bayesian procedure, which to our knowledge is a novel application in \acs{FDA}. Moreover, a key finding in our empirical studies is that a relatively low number of components is sufficient to obtain good results. This practical side of our work showcases that the Bayesian prediction methods we constructed are competitive against several (non-cherry-picked) frequentist techniques, especially those based on the usual \(L^2\)-models, while still remaining viable implementation-wise. Of course, our proposed model is not without limitations, and we are not suggesting that the \(L^2\) perspective should be abandoned, but merely offering a simple, theoretically-backed alternative that can perform better in many situations.

\section{Proofs}\label{sec:proofs-bfr}

Here we present detailed proofs of the theoretical results in this chapter. Bear in mind that the covariance function \(K\) of the process \(X\) is assumed to be strictly positive definite and continuous.

\subsection{Consistency via Doob's theorem}

For this part, the general strategy will be to apply Doob's theorem (Theorem~\ref{th:doob}) to a subset of the parameter space \(\Theta\), where permutations are suitably taken into account and full identifiability holds, and then extend the conclusions to the whole parameter space. As we will see shortly, save for an eventual permutation, identifiability of the map \(\theta \mapsto \pp_\theta(X,Y)\) is obtained in the \acs{RKHS} case when the covariance function of the underlying stochastic process is nondegenerate.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-doob-linear}]\label{proof:consistency-doob-linear}
  
We break the proof into several steps.

\paragraph{Measurability issues} We first show the measurability of the mapping \(\theta \mapsto \pp_\theta(X,Y)(A)\) for every measurable set \(A \subseteq \mathcal X \times \mathcal Y\). Define the function \(h(\theta, x, y)=f_\theta(y|x)\mathds{1}_A(x,y)\), where \(f_\theta(\cdot|x)\) is the density of the normal distribution \( \mathcal N(\alpha + \sum_j \beta_j x(t_j),\, \sigma^2)\), and \(\mathds{1}_{A}\) is the indicator function of the set \(A\). Let \(E(x,t)=x(t)\) be the evaluation map on \(\mathcal C[0,1]\times [0,1]\), and note that \(x\mapsto x(t)\) is continuous on \(\mathcal C[0,1]\) while \(t\mapsto x(t)\) is continuous on \([0,1]\). It follows that \(E\) is a Carathéodory function and hence jointly measurable \citep[][Lemma~4.51]{aliprantis2006infinite}. Thus, \(h\) is seen to be measurable as a composition of Borel measurable functions, and by Fubini/Tonelli, the function
\[
  \theta \mapsto \int_{\mathcal X \times \mathcal Y} h(\theta, x, y)\, d\qq_X(x)\,d\mathfrak{m}(y) = \pp_\theta(X, Y)(A)
\]
is measurable. When \(\sigma^2=0\) we no longer have a normal density, but a similar argument works with the corresponding Dirac measure.

Another measurability concern is the existence of regular conditional distributions such as \(\theta|(X, Y)_{1:n}\). For this, one should see Theorem 10.2.1 and Theorem 10.2.2 in \citet{dudley2002real}, which guarantee that such conditional distributions are well-defined provided that the underlying spaces are sufficiently regular.

\paragraph{Reduced parameter space} Define the constrained space
\[\tTheta_p = \left\{p\right\} \times [0,1]^p_{\text{ord}} \times \left(\R \setminus \{0\}\right)^p \times \R \times \R^+_0,
\]
where 
\[
[0,1]^p_{\text{ord}} =\left\{(t_1,\dots, t_p) \in [0,1]^p: t_1 < \cdots < t_p\right\}.
\]
Note that \(\tTheta_p\) is an open subset of \(\Theta_p\), and thus open in the full space \(\Theta\). Now consider the disjoint union \(\tTheta = \bigcup_{p\in \N}\tTheta_p\), and observe that \(\tTheta\) is a Polish space, by virtue of being open in the Polish space \(\Theta\) \citep[\S26]{bauer2001measure}, thus satisfying the first requirement of Doob's theorem.

\paragraph{Transformation into ordered form} For \(\theta\in\Theta_p\), define the measurable mapping \(T(\theta)=\theta[\nu]\) if there is a permutation \(\nu\in \mathfrak{S}_p\) such that \(\theta[\nu]\in \tTheta_p\); otherwise set \(T(\theta)=\theta\). Note that we can only transform \(\theta\) to be in \(\tTheta_p\) if the times \(t_j\) are all distinct and the coefficients \(\beta_j\) are all nonzero. But since the prior distribution assigns probability one to both events by~\ref{cond:condition-ident}, we have \(\Pi(T(\theta)\in\tTheta)=1\). It will be useful later to observe that for any set \(B\subseteq \tTheta_p\), if we denote 
\[
  B[\nu] = \left\{\theta[\nu]: \theta \in B\right\},
\]
we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inverse-image-T}
  \bigcup_{\nu\in \mathfrak{S}_p} B[\nu] = T^{-1}(B).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Collapsed model} Let \(\tilde{\Pi}\) denote the distribution of \(T(\theta)\) restricted to \(\tTheta\), and note that \(\pp_{T(\theta)}(X,Y)=\pp_\theta(X,Y)\). Then the following model holds on the reduced space \(\tTheta\):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:model-linear-collapsed}
  \begin{split}
     & T(\theta) \sim \tilde{\Pi}, \\
     & (X,Y)_{1:n}\mid T(\theta) \sim \pp_{T(\theta)}(X,Y) \quad \text{i.i.d.}
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
Observe that \(\tilde{\Pi}(B)=\Pi(T(\theta)\in B)\) for every measurable set \(B\subseteq \tTheta\). Moreover, the posterior under the original model also coincides with the posterior under the collapsed model, since \((T(\theta), (X,Y)_{1:n})\) has the same joint distribution in both models \citep[see][for details]{miller2023consistency}.

\paragraph{Verifying conditions} We will now show that the conditions of Doob's theorem hold on \(\tTheta\). First, since \(\theta \mapsto \pp_\theta(X, Y)(A)\) is measurable on \(\Theta\), it is also measurable on \(\tTheta\) for all sets \(A\subseteq \mathcal X \times \mathcal Y\) measurable. For the identifiability part, suppose by contradiction that there are two parameters \(\theta_p,\bar\theta_{\bar p} \in \tTheta\) such that \(\theta\neq \bar\theta\) and \(\pp_{\theta}(X, Y) = \pp_{\bar\theta}(X, Y)\). Then necessarily the Gaussian distributions \(\pp_{\theta}(Y|X)\) and \(\pp_{\bar\theta}(Y|X)\) coincide \(\qq_X\)-almost surely, which in turn implies that their variances agree and their means are equal almost surely, i.e., \(\sigma^2=\bar\sigma^2\) and
\[
  \alpha + \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j X(t_j) = \bar\alpha + \sum_{j=1}^{\bar p} \bar\beta_j X(\bar t_j)\quad \qq_X-\text{a.s.}
\]
Reordering the terms and combining those where the impact points coincide, we get a linear combination of marginals of the process that equals a constant:
\[
\sum_{k=1}^m \gamma_k X(s_k) = \alpha - \bar\alpha\quad \qq_X-\text{a.s.},
\]
where \(s_1,\dots,s_m\) are distinct times. By taking variances, we can see that all the coefficients in this linear combination must vanish (and hence \(\alpha=\bar\alpha\)), since the covariance function of the process is strictly positive definite. But \(\beta_j\) and \(\bar\beta_j\) cannot be zero for any \(j\) by definition of \(\tTheta\), so it must be the case that \(p=\bar p\) and \(\bar\theta=\theta[\nu]\) for some \(\nu \in \mathfrak{S}_p\). However, \(t_1< \cdots < t_p\) and \(\bar t_1 < \cdots < \bar t_p\), so  \(\nu\) must be the identity permutation, that is, \(\bar\theta=\theta\), contradicting the initial assumption.

\paragraph{Applying Doob's theorem} Next we analyze the conclusions of Doob's theorem applied to the collapsed model~\eqref{eq:model-linear-collapsed}: there exists a subset \(\tTheta_* \subseteq \tTheta\) with \(\Pi(T(\theta)\in\tTheta_*)=1\) such that, if \(T(\theta_0)\in \tTheta_*\) and \((X,Y)_{1:\infty} \sim \pp_{T(\theta_0)}(X, Y)\) i.i.d., then for any neighborhood \(B\subseteq \tTheta\) of \(T(\theta_0)\) it holds that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:consistency-collapsed}
  \Pi_n\left(T(\theta) \in B \mid (X,Y)_{1:n}\right) \xrightarrow[]{n\to\infty} 1 \quad \pp_{T(\theta_0)}^\infty(X, Y)-\text{a.s.}
\end{equation}
Now define \(\Theta_*\) to be the set of all parameters in \(\Theta\) that can be obtained by permuting a parameter in \(\tTheta_*\), i.e., 
\[
  \Theta_* := \bigcup_{p=1}^\infty \bigcup_{\nu\in \mathfrak{S}_p}\left(\tTheta_* \cap \tTheta_p\right)[\nu].
\]
Then, by~\eqref{eq:inverse-image-T} we have
\[
  \Pi(\theta\in \Theta_*) = \Pi\left(T(\theta) \in \bigcup_{p=1}^\infty (\tTheta_*\cap \tTheta_p)\right) = \Pi(T(\theta)\in \tTheta_*) = 1.
\]

\paragraph{Extending the result} Let \(\theta_0\in\Theta_*\) be the true parameter, so that \((X,Y)_{1:\infty}\sim \pp_{\theta_0}(X, Y)\) i.i.d. Fix \(\epsilon\in(0,1)\) and consider the set \(B_0^\epsilon\) of all ordered parameters that are within \(\epsilon\) of the ordered version of \(\theta_0\):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:set-B}
  B_0^\epsilon = \left\{ \theta \in \tTheta: d_\Theta(T(\theta_0), \theta) < \epsilon \right\}.
\end{equation}
Observe that, since \(\epsilon < 1\), we have \(B_0^\epsilon\subseteq \tTheta_{p_0}\) by definition of \(d_\Theta\); see~\eqref{eq:metric-doob}. Moreover, by~\eqref{eq:neighborhood-doob-permutation} it holds that
\[
  \bigcup_{\nu\in \mathfrak{S}_{0}}B_0^\epsilon[\nu]\subseteq B_{d_\Theta}^{\mathfrak{S}}(\theta_0, \epsilon).
\]  
Then, again by~\eqref{eq:inverse-image-T}, we can write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:consistency-intermediate}
  \begin{split}
    \Pi_n\left(\theta \in B_{d_\Theta}^{\mathfrak{S}}(\theta_0, \epsilon) \mid (X,Y)_{1:n}\right) & \geq \Pi_n\left(\theta \in \bigcup_{\nu\in \mathfrak{S}_{0}} B_0^\epsilon[\nu] \mid (X,Y)_{1:n}\right) \\
    & = \Pi_n\left(T(\theta) \in B_0^\epsilon \mid (X,Y)_{1:n}\right).
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
Now, \(T(\theta_0)\in{\tTheta_*}\) because \(\theta_0\in\Theta_*\), and in that case we know that the collapsed model is consistent at \(T(\theta_0)\). Note that we also have \((X,Y)_{1:\infty} \sim \pp_{T(\theta_0)}(X, Y)\) i.i.d.\ (since \(\pp_{\theta_0}=\pp_{T(\theta_0)}\)), and the set \(B_0^\epsilon\) in~\eqref{eq:set-B} is a neighborhood of \(T(\theta_0)\) in \(\tTheta\). Then, by~\eqref{eq:consistency-collapsed} we can conclude that 
\[
  \lim_{n\to\infty} \Pi_n\left(T(\theta) \in B_0^\epsilon \mid (X,Y)_{1:n}\right) = 1 \quad \pp_0^\infty (X,Y)-\text{a.s.},
\]
and this fact together with~\eqref{eq:consistency-intermediate} proves consistency for \(\theta_0\) in the original model~\eqref{eq:model-linear}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:consistency-lebesgue-linear}]\label{proof:consistency-lebesgue-linear}
  
Define \(\Theta_*\) as in the proof of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-doob-linear} above, and consider for \(p\in\N\) the measurable set \(B_p=\Theta_p \setminus \Theta_*\subseteq \Theta_p\). Since \(\Pi(\Theta \setminus \Theta_*)=0\), we necessarily have \(\Pi(B_p)=0\). Moreover, by the permutation-invariance of \(\Theta_*\) it holds that \(B_p[\nu] = B_p\) for all \(\nu \in \mathfrak{S}_p\). Hence,
\[
\sum_{\nu\in \mathfrak{S}_p} \Pi(\theta[\nu] \in B_p) = p! \cdot \Pi(B_p) = 0.
\]
Then, by assumption, \(\mathfrak{m}_{2p+2}(B_p)=0\). But this is valid for all \(p\in\N\), so we can conclude that 
\[
\mathfrak{m}_\infty(\Theta\setminus \Theta_*) = \sum_{p=1}^\infty \mathfrak{m}_{2p+2}(B_p)=0.
\]
\end{proof}

\subsection{Consistency and contraction rates via Schwartz's theorem}

We begin by addressing the claim that \(f_\theta(y|x)\) is a joint density of \((X,Y)\) with respect to \(\rho=\qq_X\times \mathfrak{m}\), as long as \(\sigma^2>0\).

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lemma:joint-density-rho}]\label{proof:joint-density-rho}
First, consider a measurable rectangle \(A_X\times A_Y\) in the product (Borel) \(\sigma\)-algebra of \(\mathcal X\) and \(\mathcal Y\). Then, since both \(\mathcal X\) and \(\mathcal Y\) are Polish spaces, by the disintegration theorem \citep[e.g.][\S10.6]{bogachev2007measure} and Fubini/Tonelli, we have
\begin{align*}
  \pp(X\in A_X, Y\in A_Y) &= \int_{A_X} \left(\int_{A_Y} f_\theta(y|x)\, d\mathfrak{m}(y)\right) d\qq_X(x)\\
  &= \int_{A_X\times A_Y} f_\theta(y|x)\, d\rho(x, y).
\end{align*}
Now extend the argument to a general measurable set \(A\subseteq \mathcal X \times \mathcal Y\) by a standard application of Dynkin's \(\pi\)-\(\lambda\) theorem \citep[Lemma~4.11]{aliprantis2006infinite}, where the \(\pi\)-system is the class of measurable rectangles and the \(\lambda\)-system is the class of measurable sets that verify the intended condition.
\end{proof}

Recall that our full parameter space is the disjoint union \(\Theta=\bigcup_p\Theta_p\). As we said before, for convenience we take the finite-dimensional sets \(\Theta_p\) to be compact spaces. More specifically, we work with
\[
  \Theta_p = \left\{p\right\} \times [0,1]^p \times [-M_B, M_B]^p \times [-M_A, M_A] \times [\sigma^2_{\text{min}}, \sigma^2_{\text{max}}],
\]
where \(M_A,M_B>0\) and \(0<\sigma^2_{\text{min}}<\sigma^2_{\text{max}}\). Sometimes we will consider only the continuous components of \(\Theta_p\) (discarding the discrete label), but we keep the same notation for simplicity.

Next, we present a collection of standard results on covering numbers that will be extensively used in the subsequent proofs.

\begin{lemma}[see \citealp{vaart2023weak}]\label{lemma:covering-number}
  Suppose \(d\) is a distance on the class of densities \(\mathcal F=\{f_\theta: \theta\in\Theta\}\).
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item If \(\epsilon\leq \tilde\epsilon\) then \(N(\tilde\epsilon, \mathcal F, d) \leq N(\epsilon, \mathcal F, d)\).
    \item If \(\tilde d\) is another distance on \(\mathcal F\) such that \(d\leq \tilde C \tilde d\) for some \(\tilde C > 0\), then \(N(\epsilon, \mathcal F, d) \leq N(\epsilon/\tilde C, \mathcal F, \tilde d)\) for all \(\epsilon > 0\).
    \item If there is a constant \(\tilde C>0\) such that \(d(f_{\theta}, f_{\tilde\theta})\leq \tilde C \|\theta - \tilde\theta\|\) for all \(\theta, \tilde\theta \in  \Theta\), then \(N(\epsilon, \mathcal F, d)\leq N(\epsilon/\tilde C, \Theta, \|\cdot\|)\) for all \(\epsilon > 0\). % Theorem 2.7.17 in van der Vaart and Wellner (1996)
    \item If \(A\subset \R^D\) has finite diameter \(R_D\), then \(N(\epsilon, A, \|\cdot\|) \leq (3R_D/\epsilon)^D\) for all \(0<\epsilon<R_D\). % Proposition C.2 in Ghosal and van der Vaart (2017)
  \end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

Additionally, for a given parameter \(\theta\in\Theta\) we consider the measurable mean function \(\mu_\theta:\mathcal C[0,1]\to \R\) given by 
\[
  \mu_\theta(x)=\alpha + \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j x(t_j),
\] 
which allows us to express the density of \(Y|X=x, \theta\) in our linear model as the normal density
\[
  f_\theta(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(y-\mu_\theta(x))^2}{2\sigma^2}\right\}.
\]

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-schwartz-linear}]\label{proof:consistency-schwartz-linear}

We need to verify the conditions of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-hellinger}. As was previously announced, we will be using the sieve of parameters \(\Theta_n = \bigcup_{p=1}^{p_n} \Theta_p\), and the corresponding sieve of densities given by \(\mathcal F_n=\{f_\theta\in \mathcal F: \theta \in \Theta_n\}\). The idea is to select a suitable growth order for the upper limit \(p_n\) in the sieve so that all conditions hold.

\paragraph{Bounding the metric entropy} First, we claim that there exists \(C^{(p)}>0\) so that \(d_H(f_\theta, f_{\tilde\theta})\leq C^{(p)}\|\theta - \tilde\theta\|\) for all \(\theta, \tilde\theta\) in a fixed (finite-dimensional) \(\Theta_p\). Starting with the function \(\mu_\theta\) and a given trajectory \(x\) of \(X\), rewrite the absolute difference in means as
\[
  |\mu_\theta(x) - \mu_{\tilde\theta}(x)| = \left| (\alpha - \tilde\alpha) + \sum_{j=1}^p \left(\beta_j (x(t_j) - x(\tilde t_{j}))+ (\beta_j - \tilde\beta_{j})x(\tilde t_{j})\right) \right|.
\]
Now from the triangle inequality, the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality and the fact that \((a+b)^2 \leq 2(a^2 + b^2)\), we have
\begin{align*}
  \frac{1}{2}(\mu_\theta(x) - \mu_{\tilde\theta}(x))^2 &\leq (\alpha - \tilde\alpha)^2 + 2p\biggl(M_B^2\sum_{j=1}^p (x(t_j) - x(\tilde t_j))^2\\
  &\quad + \sum_{j=1}^p x(\tilde t_j)^2(\beta_j - \tilde\beta_j)^2\biggr).
\end{align*}
Now integrate on both sides with respect to \(\qq_X\). By~\ref{cond:condition-schwartz-X}, we have
\[
\int_{\mathcal X} (x(t_j) - x(\tilde t_j))^2 d\qq_X(x) = \E\left[(X(t_j) - X(\tilde t_j))^2\right] \leq L(t_j - \tilde t_j)^2.
\]
Moreover, note that \(\|\theta-\tilde\theta\|^2\) is always bigger than the sum of just some of the squared components of \(\theta - \tilde\theta\). All in all, we get
\begin{align*}
  \frac{1}{2}\E\left[(\mu_\theta(X) - \mu_{\tilde\theta}(X))^2\right] &\leq \|\theta - \tilde\theta\|^2 + 2p\biggl(M_B^2 L \|\theta - \tilde\theta\|^2\\ 
  &\quad + \|\theta - \tilde\theta\|^2 \sup_{t\in[0,1]} \E\left[X(t)^2\right] \biggr).
\end{align*}
Recall that the covariance function \(K\) of \(X\) is assumed to be continuous, so \(\sup_{t\in[0,1]} \E[X(t)^2]=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}K(t,t)<\infty\). Then, we can bring all constants together (which are positive and depend on \(M_B\), \(p\) and \(X\)) into \(C_1>0\) to write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:th9-bound1}
  \E\left[(\mu_\theta(X) - \mu_{\tilde\theta}(X))^2\right] \leq C_1 \|\theta - \tilde\theta\|^2.
\end{equation}
Next, we compute \(d^2_H(f_\theta, f_{\tilde\theta})\). First, define the auxiliary function
\[
  S(\sigma, \tilde\sigma)= \sqrt{\frac{2\sigma \tilde\sigma}{\sigma^2 + \tilde\sigma^2}}.
\]
Using the inequality \(e^{-a}\geq 1-a\) for \(a\geq 0\) and the well-known expression for the Hellinger distance between two univariate normal distributions \citep[e.g.][p.~51]{pardo2018statistical}, we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:th9-bound2}
\begin{split}
  d^2_H(f_\theta, f_{\tilde\theta}) & = 1 - \bigintssss_{\mathcal X} \left(\bigintssss_{\mathcal Y}\sqrt{f_\theta(y|x) f_{\tilde\theta}(y|x)}\, d\mathfrak{m}(y)\right) d\qq_X(x)\\
  & = 1 - S(\sigma, \tilde\sigma)\bigintsss_{\mathcal X} \exp\left\{ -\frac{(\mu_\theta(x) - \mu_{\tilde\theta}(x))^2}{4(\sigma^2 + \tilde\sigma^2)} \right\} d\qq_X(x)                                         \\
  & \leq 1 - S(\sigma, \tilde\sigma) + S(\sigma, \tilde\sigma) \bigintsss_{\mathcal X} \frac{(\mu_\theta(x) - \mu_{\tilde\theta}(x))^2}{4(\sigma^2+\tilde\sigma^2)}\, d\qq_X(x) \\
  & \leq C_2\|\theta - \tilde\theta\|^2 + C_3 \E\left[(\mu_\theta(X) - \mu_{\tilde\theta}(X))^2\right],
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where \(C_2\) and \(C_3\) are positive constants that depend on \(\sigma^2_{\text{min}}\) and \(\sigma^2_{\text{max}}\). Obtaining \(C_3\) is straightforward; to get \(C_2\), let \(u=\sigma^2\), \(v=\tilde\sigma^2\) and \(S=\sqrt{\frac{2\sqrt{uv}}{u+v}}\), and note that
\[
  1-S = \frac{1-S^2}{1+S} \leq 1 - S^2.
\]
Now, a bit of algebraic manipulation yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:th9-computation-S}
  1 - S^2 = 1 - \frac{2\sqrt{uv}}{u+v} = \frac{(\sqrt{u}-\sqrt{v})^2}{u+v} = \frac{(u-v)^2}{(u+v)(\sqrt{u} + \sqrt{v})^2},
\end{equation}
and after reverting the change of variables the denominator can be easily bounded in terms of \(\sigma^2_{\text{min}}\) and \(\sigma^2_{\text{max}}\). To conclude, combine~\eqref{eq:th9-bound2} with the previous bound~\eqref{eq:th9-bound1} to get the desired inequality
\begin{equation}\label{eq:th9-bound3}
  d^2_H(f_\theta, f_{\tilde\theta}) \leq C^{(p)} \|\theta - \tilde\theta\|^2,
\end{equation}
where \(C^{(p)}>0\) increases linearly with \(p\).
Next, consider the distance \(d_\Theta\) on \(\Theta\) defined in~\eqref{eq:metric-doob}. From~\eqref{eq:th9-bound3} one can readily verify that \(d_H(f_\theta, f_{\tilde\theta}) \leq C(n) d_\Theta(\theta, \tilde\theta)\) for \(\theta, \tilde\theta \in \Theta_n\), where \(C(n)=O(\sqrt{p_n})\). Moreover, the relationship \(d_\Theta(\theta,\tilde\theta)\leq \|\theta - \tilde\theta\|\) always holds on \(\Theta_p\). Denote by \(R_p\) the diameter of \((\Theta_p, \|\cdot\|)\), and note that each \(\Theta_p\) is contained in a hypercube of dimension \(2p+2\) and length independent of \(p\), say \(\ell\). Then, \(R_p\leq \ell\sqrt{2p+2}\) for all \(p\). Putting it all together and invoking Lemma~\ref{lemma:covering-number}, for sufficiently small \(\epsilon>0\) we have
\begin{align*}
  N(\epsilon, \mathcal F_n, d_H) & \leq N\left(\epsilon/C(n), \Theta_n, d_\Theta\right)                               \\
  & \leq \sum_{p=1}^{p_n} N\left(\epsilon/C(n), \Theta_p, \|\cdot\|\right)             \\
  & \leq p_n \left(\frac{3C(n)\ell\sqrt{2p_n+2}}{\epsilon}\right)^{2p_n+2}.
\end{align*}
Taking logarithms, we may write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:th9-bound-covering}
  \log N(\epsilon, \mathcal F_n, d_H) \leq \log p_n + (2p_n+2) \log\left(\frac{3C(n) \ell\sqrt{2p_n+2}}{\epsilon}\right).
\end{equation}
Since \(C(n)=O(\sqrt{p_n})\), it suffices to choose \(p_n\) so that \(p_n\log p_n = o(n)\), or equivalently, \(p_n=o\left(n/\log n\right)\). In this way the right-hand side is eventually less than \(n\epsilon^2\), satisfying the first condition of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-hellinger}.

\paragraph{Bounding the prior tail mass} We need to show that the tail mass of the prior decays at least exponentially with \(n\). An initial crude bound on this tail mass in the parameter space is
\begin{align*}
  \Pi(\theta \in \Theta \setminus \Theta_n) &= \Pi\left(\theta \in \bigcup_{p=p_n + 1}^\infty \Theta_p\right)\\
  &= \sum_{p=p_n+1}^\infty \Pi(\theta \in \Theta_p)\\
  &\leq \sum_{p=p_n+1}^{\infty} \Pi(p).
\end{align*}
Since \(\Pi(p)\lesssim \exp\{-\delta p(\log p)^k\}\) for sufficiently large \(p\) and multiplicative constants do not affect the bound, we can work without loss of generality with \(\Pi(p)=\exp\{-\delta p(\log p)^k\}\). Then, we have
\begin{align*}
  \Pi(\Theta \setminus \Theta_n) & \leq \sum_{p=p_n+1}^\infty \exp\left\{-\delta p(\log p)^k\right\}                                \\
  & = \sum_{m=1}^\infty \exp\left\{-\delta (p_n+m)(\log (p_n+m))^k\right\}                           \\
  & \leq \sum_{m=1}^\infty \exp\left\{-\delta p_n (\log p_n)^k\right\}\exp\left\{-\delta m (\log p_n)^k\right\} \\
  & = \exp\left\{-\delta p_n (\log p_n)^k\right\} \sum_{m=1}^\infty r_n^m,
\end{align*}
where \(r_n=\exp\{-\delta (\log p_n)^k\}<1\). Summing the geometric series, we get
\begin{equation}\label{eq:proof-infinite-prior-tail-bound}
  \Pi(\Theta \setminus \Theta_n) \leq \exp\left\{-\delta p_n (\log p_n)^k\right\} \frac{\exp\left\{-\delta (\log p_n)^k\right\}}{1-\exp\left\{-\delta (\log p_n)^k\right\}}.
\end{equation}
Now choose \(p_n \geq \frac{c_1n}{(\log n)^k}\) with \(c_1>0\), and observe that for sufficiently large \(n\) we have
\[
  \log p_n \geq \log(c_1) + \log n - k\log \log n \geq \frac{1}{2}\log n.
\]
Since \(p_n\to\infty\), the fraction in~\eqref{eq:proof-infinite-prior-tail-bound} goes to zero as \(n\) grows, and there exists a constant \(c_2>0\) such that, for large \(n\),
\[
  \frac{\exp\left\{-\delta (\log p_n)^k\right\}}{1-\exp\left\{-\delta (\log p_n)^k\right\}}\leq c_2.
\]
Then, using the lower bounds on \(p_n\) and \(\log p_n\) it follows that eventually
\begin{align*}
  \Pi(\Theta \setminus \Theta_n) & \leq c_2 \exp\left\{-\delta p_n (\log p_n)^k\right\}                                                    \\
                                 & \leq c_2 \exp\left\{-\delta \frac{c_1 n}{(\log n)^k}\left(\frac{1}{2}\log n\right)^k\right\} \\
                                 & = c_2 \exp\left\{-\tilde{C}n\right\}.
\end{align*}
As we said before, multiplicative constants do not significantly affect the bound, so we can absorb \(c_2\) into the exponential. Indeed, observe that \(c_2\leq e^{\xi n}\) for any \(\xi>0\) and \(n\) sufficiently large. Then, we have \(c_2e^{-\tilde{C}n} \leq e^{-(\tilde{C}-\xi)n}\), so it suffices to choose \(C=\tilde{C}-\xi\) with \(\xi\) small enough so that \(C>0\). We still need to translate the bound to the prior on \(\mathcal F \setminus \mathcal F_n\), but this is immediate, since
\[
  \Pi_{\mathcal F}(\mathcal F \setminus \mathcal F_n) = \Pi(\theta\in\Theta: f_\theta \in \mathcal F \setminus \mathcal F_n) \leq \Pi(\Theta \setminus \Theta_n).
\]
Note that the lower bound \(p_n \gtrsim \frac{n}{(\log n)^k}\) does not contradict the upper bound \(p_n=o(n/\log n)\) needed for the metric entropy condition above. Hence, we can choose an appropriate growth rate for \(p_n\) in our sieve to make both conditions hold simultaneously.

\paragraph{Verifying the KL support} First, we proceed by directly computing \(\dkl(f_0 \,\|\, f_\theta)\) for \(\theta\in\Theta_0\), which again has a familiar formula in the normal case \citep[e.g.][p.~47]{pardo2018statistical}, and bounding the result by using~\eqref{eq:th9-bound1}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:th9-bound4}
  \begin{split}
  \dkl(f_0 \,\|\, f_\theta) & =  \bigintssss_{\mathcal X} \left( \bigintssss_{\mathcal Y}f_0(y|x)\log\left(\frac{f_0(y|x)}{f_\theta(y|x)}\right)  d\mathfrak{m}(y)\right)d\qq_X(x)\\
  & = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\sigma_0^2}{\sigma^2} - 1 - \log \frac{\sigma_0^2}{\sigma^2} \right)\\
  &\quad + \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\bigintssss_{\mathcal X} (\mu_0(x) - \mu_\theta(x))^2\, d\qq_X(x)\\
  & \leq C_1\|\theta_0 - \theta\|^2 + C_2\|\theta_0-\theta\|^2 \\
  & = \tilde C\|\theta_0 - \theta\|^2,
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
where \(\mu_0(x) := \mu_{\theta_0}(x)\). To obtain \(C_1\), let \(I=[\sigma^2_{\text{min}}/\sigma^2_\text{max},\, \sigma^2_{\text{max}}/\sigma^2_\text{min}]\) and \(u=\sigma_0^2/\sigma^2\), and consider the function \(g(u)=u - 1 - \log u\) for \(u\in I\). Define the auxiliary function \(h(u) = g(u)/(u-1)^2\) for \(u\neq 1\) and \(h(1)=1/2\). It is immediate to show that \(h\) is continuous on \(I\), and thus it attains its maximum value
\[H :=\max_{u \in I} h(u)\geq 1/2 > 0.
\]
It follows that \(g(u) \leq H (u-1)^2\) for \(u\in I\), which gives us the desired bound after undoing the change of variables. Then, from~\eqref{eq:th9-bound4} and the behavior of \(\Pi\) on \(\Theta_0\) we can conclude that \(f_0\in\kl(\Pi_{\mathcal F})\), since for every \(\epsilon>0\) we have
\begin{align*}
  \Pi_{\mathcal F}\left(f_\theta\in \mathcal F: \dkl(f_0 \,\|\, f_\theta) < \epsilon\right) & =\Pi\left(\theta \in \Theta: \dkl(f_0 \,\|\, f_\theta) < \epsilon\right)      \\
  & \geq \Pi\left(\theta \in \Theta_0: \dkl(f_0 \,\|\, f_\theta) <\epsilon\right) \\
  & \geq \Pi\left(\theta\in\Theta_0: \|\theta_0 - \theta\|^2 < \epsilon/\tilde C\right)\\
  & > 0.
\end{align*}
\end{proof}

To prove the theorem concerning the contraction rate, we will use the following auxiliary result.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:th11-lemma-lebesgue}
  If the prior \(\Pi\) has a density on \(\Theta_0\) with respect to Lebesgue measure that is bounded away from zero in a neighborhood of \(\theta_0\), then there is a constant \(c>0\) such that \(\Pi(B(\theta_0, r))\geq cr^{2p_0+2}\) for all sufficiently small \(r>0\).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Let \(g\) be the density of \(\Pi\) restricted to \(\Theta_0\), and let \(r_0>0\) be a radius such that \(g(\theta)\geq g_0>0\) for all \(\theta\in B(\theta_0, r_0)\). Then, if \(q=2p_0+2\), for every \(0<r\leq r_0\) we have
  \[
    \Pi(B(\theta_0, r)) =\int_{B(\theta_0, r)} g\, d\mathfrak{m}_q \geq g_0 \mathfrak{m}_q(B(\theta_0, r)) = g_0 c_q r^q,
  \]
  where \(\mathfrak{m}_q\) is the Lebesgue measure on \(\Theta_0\) and \(c_q>0\) is a constant that depends only on the dimension of \(\Theta_0\).
\end{proof}

Now we will show that the sequence \(\epsilon_n=Dn^{-\gamma}\) with \(0<\gamma<1/2\) and a certain \(D>0\) satisfies the conditions of Theorem~\ref{th:contraction-rate}, and thus is a posterior contraction rate for our model. Note that the specific value of \(D\) is of no relevance, since it is absorbed into the diverging sequence \(M_n\) in the definition of a contraction rate.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{th:contraction-rate-schwartz-linear}]\label{proof:contraction-rate-schwartz-linear}

This time we will consider the same type of sieve as before, namely \(\Theta_n = \bigcup_{p=1}^{p_n}\Theta_p\), but with an upper limit given by
\[
  p_n=\left\lfloor\frac{n^{1-2\gamma}}{\log n}\right\rfloor,
\]
where \(\lfloor \cdot \rfloor\) is the floor function. As we will see, the conditions imposed in the statement of Theorem~\ref{th:contraction-rate-schwartz-linear} are precisely the ones needed to ensure that the corresponding sieve of densities \(\mathcal F_n=\{f_\theta\in\mathcal F: \theta \in \Theta_n\}\) satisfies the requirements of Theorem~\ref{th:contraction-rate}.

\paragraph{Bounding the metric entropy} From the reasoning in~\eqref{eq:th9-bound-covering} in the proof of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-schwartz-linear}, we have the bound
\[
  \log N(\epsilon_n/2, \mathcal F_n, d_H)  \leq \log p_n + (2p_n+2)\log\left(\frac{6C(n) \ell\sqrt{2p_n+2}}{\epsilon_n}\right).
\]
Since \(\epsilon_n=Dn^{-\gamma}\) and \(C(n)\lesssim  \sqrt{p_n}\) eventually, we can absorb all constants inside the second logarithm into \(c_1>0\). Indeed, for sufficiently large \(n\) we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:th11-bound1}
  \log N(\epsilon_n/2, \mathcal F_n, d_H)  \leq \log p_n + (2p_n+2)\log\left(c_1 p_n n^\gamma\right).
\end{equation}
Observe that \(p_n\leq \frac{n^{1-2\gamma}}{\log n}\) by the upper limit imposed earlier, so substituting into~\eqref{eq:th11-bound1} yields
\begin{align*}
  \log N(\epsilon_n/2, \mathcal F_n, d_H) & \leq \log \left(\frac{n^{1-2\gamma}}{\log n}\right)\\
  &\quad + \left(\frac{2n^{1-2\gamma}}{\log n} + 2\right)\left[\log c_1 + \log\left(\frac{n^{1-\gamma}}{\log n}\right)\right].
\end{align*}
Simplifying the expression in the right-hand side, the dominant term for large \(n\) is \(2(1-\gamma)n^{1-2\gamma}\), and the rest of the terms are of order strictly smaller. This means that there exists a constant \(c_2>0\) as small as we want such that every term apart from the dominant term is eventually smaller than \(c_2 n^{1-2\gamma}\). Thus, for sufficiently large \(n\) we can write
\[
  \log N(\epsilon_n/2, \mathcal F_n, d_H)  \leq  (c_2 + 2-2\gamma)n^{1-2\gamma}.
\]
Since \(n\epsilon_n^2 =D^2n^{1-2\gamma}\), the conclusion follows for \(D^2>2-2\gamma\).

\paragraph{Bounding the prior tail mass} Consider without loss of generality that \(\Pi(p)=c_1\exp\{-\delta p \log p\}\). Then, we have
\begin{align*}
  \Pi(\Theta \setminus \Theta_n) & \leq \sum_{p>p_n} c_1\exp\left\{-\delta p \log p\right\}                                                        \\
  & = c_1\sum_{m=1}^\infty \exp\left\{-\delta (p_n+m) \log(p_n+m)\right\}                                           \\
  & \leq c_1\sum_{m=1}^\infty  \exp\left\{-\delta(p_n+m)\log p_n\right\}                                            \\
  & \leq c_1\exp\left\{-\delta p_n \log p_n\right\} \sum_{m=1}^\infty \left(\exp\left\{-\delta \log p_n\right\}\right)^m       \\
  & = c_1\exp\left\{-\delta p_n \log p_n\right\} \frac{\exp\left\{-\delta \log p_n\right\}}{1-\exp\left\{-\delta \log p_n\right\}}.
\end{align*}
Now, since \(p_n\geq \frac{n^{1-2\gamma}}{2\log n}\), for large \(n\) we have
\begin{align*}
  \log p_n & \geq (1-2\gamma)\log n - \log (2\log n) \geq \frac{1-2\gamma}{2}\log n.
\end{align*}
Since \(p_n\to\infty\), there exists a constant \(c_2>0\) such that
\[
  \frac{\exp\left\{-\delta \log p_n\right\}}{1-\exp\left\{-\delta \log p_n\right\}}\leq c_2\quad \text{for large } n.
\]
Then, using the lower bounds on \(p_n\) and \(\log p_n\) it follows that
\[
  \Pi(\Theta \setminus \Theta_n) \leq c_1c_2 \exp\left\{\frac{-\delta (1-2\gamma)}{4}n^{1-2\gamma}\right\}.
\]
At this point we integrate \(c_1c_2\) into the exponential, similar to what we did before. Let \(c_3=\delta(1-2\gamma)/4>0\) and choose \(0<\xi<c_3\) such that \(c_1c_2\leq \exp\{\xi n^{1-2\gamma}\}\) for sufficiently large \(n\). Then, if \(c_4=c_3-\xi\), we have \(\Pi(\Theta \setminus \Theta_n) \leq \exp\{-c_4n^{1-2\gamma}\}\). Now, observe that 
\[
  (C+4)n\epsilon_n^2=(C+4)D^2n^{1-2\gamma},
\]
where \(C\) is the constant appearing in condition (ii) of Theorem~\ref{th:contraction-rate}, so it suffices to choose \(C\leq c_4/D^2 -4\) and we are done. It can be easily verified that this upper bound on \(C\) is positive as long as \(\xi\) is chosen small enough:
\[
\frac{c_4}{D^2} - 4 > 0 \iff 4D^2 < \frac{\delta(1-2\gamma)}{4} \iff D^2 < \frac{\delta(1-2\gamma)}{16}.
\]
On the other hand, the lower bound \(D^2>2-2\gamma\) imposed earlier gives
\[
\frac{\delta(1-2\gamma)}{16} > 2-2\gamma \iff \delta > \frac{32(1-\gamma)}{1-2\gamma},
\]
which is precisely our assumption on \(\delta\).

\paragraph{Sufficient prior mass around the true density} Let \(\theta\in\Theta_0\). We know from~\eqref{eq:th9-bound4} in the proof of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-schwartz-linear} that there exists a constant \(C_1>0\) such that \(\dkl(f_0 \,\|\, f_\theta)\leq C_1^2 \|\theta_0 - \theta\|^2\). On the other hand, direct calculation \citep[see e.g.][]{choi2007posterior} shows that, under Gaussianity,
\[
  V_2(f_0, f_\theta) = \frac{(\sigma_0^2 - \sigma^2)^2}{2\sigma^4} + \frac{\sigma_0^2}{\sigma^4}\bigintsss_{\mathcal X} (\mu_0(x) - \mu_\theta(x))^2\, d\qq_X(x).
\]
Following the same reasoning as we did multiple times before, we can see that \(V_2(f_0, f_\theta)\) is bounded above by \(C_2^2\|\theta_0 - \theta\|^2\) with \(C_2>0\). Therefore, if \(\|\theta_0 - \theta\| < \epsilon_n/\tilde C\) where \(\tilde C = \max\{C_1, C_2\}\), it follows that \(\dkl(f_0 \,\|\, f_\theta)< \epsilon_n^2\) and \(V_2(f_0, f_\theta) < \epsilon_n^2\). Thus, if we denote by \(B(\theta_0, \epsilon)\) the open ball of radius \(\epsilon\) centered at \(\theta_0\) in \(\Theta_0\), we have just shown that \(\theta \in B(\theta_0,\epsilon_n/\tilde{C})\) implies \(f_\theta \in B_2(f_0, \epsilon_n)\), and hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:th11-bound2}
  \Pi_{\mathcal F}(B_2(f_0, \epsilon_n)) \geq \Pi(B(\theta_0,\epsilon_n/\tilde{C})).
\end{equation}
Applying Lemma~\ref{lemma:th11-lemma-lebesgue} with \(r=\epsilon_n/\tilde C=Dn^{-\gamma}/\tilde C\) and taking logarithms in~\eqref{eq:th11-bound2}, we have, for large \(n\),
\begin{align*}
  \log \Pi_{\mathcal F}(B_2(f_0, \epsilon_n)) &\geq \log c + (2p_0+2)\log\left(\frac{Dn^{-\gamma}}{\tilde C}\right) \\
  &\geq \tilde c -\gamma(2p_0 + 2)\log n\\
  &\geq -\gamma 4p_0\log n,
\end{align*}
where \(\tilde c > 0\) is an irrelevant constant in the asymptotic regime. To finish the proof we want to find a constant \(C>0\) such that 
\[
  \log \Pi_{\mathcal F}(B_2(f_0, \epsilon_n)) \geq - Cn\epsilon_n^2=-CD^2n^{1-2\gamma}.
\]
But, since \(\log n\) grows much slower than \(n^{1-2\gamma}\), the inequality 
\[
  -\gamma 4p_0\log n \geq -CD^2n^{1-2\gamma}
\]
holds for any choice of \(C>0\) and sufficiently large \(n\).
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{corollary:contraction-rate-schwartz-linear-2}]\label{proof:contraction-rate-schwartz-linear-2}
We simply use the fact that, if \(d\) is another distance on \(\mathcal F\) with \(d\leq \tilde C d_H\), then
\[
  \Pi_n\left(\theta: d(f_0, f_\theta)>\epsilon\mid \text{data}\right) \leq \Pi_n\left(\theta: d_H(f_0, f_\theta)>\epsilon/\tilde C\mid \text{data}\right).
\]

For the \(L^1\)-distance, by the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality we have
\begin{align*}
  d_1^2(f_0, f_\theta) & = \left(\int |f_0 - f_\theta|\, d\rho\right)^2                                                                          \\
                       & = \left(\int \left|\sqrt{f_0} - \sqrt{f_\theta}\right|\left(\sqrt{f_0} + \sqrt{f_\theta}\right) d\rho\right)^2          \\
                       & \leq \int\left(\sqrt{f_0} - \sqrt{f_\theta}\right)^2 d\rho \cdot \int \left(\sqrt{f_0} + \sqrt{f_\theta}\right)^2 d\rho \\
                       & \leq 8 d^2_H(f_0, f_\theta).
\end{align*}
In the last step we have used the alternative definition of the Hellinger distance, namely 
\[
  d_H^2(f_0, f_\theta) = \frac{1}{2}\bigintssss\left(\sqrt{f_0} - \sqrt{f_\theta}\right)^2 d\rho, 
\] 
the basic inequality \((a + b)^2 \leq 2(a^2 + b^2)\), and the fact that both \(f_0\) and \(f_\theta\) integrate to unity over the whole space.

For the mean-variance discrepancy distance, recall the expression for the squared Hellinger distance between two normal densities, which can be formulated in this case as \(d^2_H(f_0, f_\theta)= \int g_{0,\theta}(x)\, d\qq_X(x)\), with
\[
  g_{0,\theta}(x) = 1 - \sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_0\sigma}{\sigma_0^2 + \sigma^2}}\exp\left\{ -\frac{(\mu_0(x) - \mu_{\theta}(x))^2}{4(\sigma_0^2 + \sigma^2)} \right\}.
\]
Define \(\Delta_\mu = \mu_0-\mu_\theta\), \(\Delta_{\mu_M} = [\mu_0]_M - [\mu_\theta]_M\) (\(M>0\)) and \(S=\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_0\sigma}{\sigma_0^2+\sigma^2}}\). On the one hand, using the trivial bounds \(e^{-a}\leq 1\) and \(S\leq 1\), we have
\[
  g_{0,\theta}(x) \geq 1 - S = \frac{1-S^2}{1+S} \geq \frac{1-S^2}{2} \geq C_1(\sigma_0^2 - \sigma^2)^2,
\]
where the last inequality comes from the computations in~\eqref{eq:th9-computation-S}. On the other hand, observe that \(\Delta^2_\mu \geq \Delta^2_{\mu_M}\) and \(\Delta^2_{\mu_M}\leq 4M^2\). Use these facts together with the bound \(1-e^{-a}\geq a/(1+a)\) for \(a\geq 0\) to get
\[
  g_{0,\theta}(x)  \geq  1 - \exp\left\{ -\frac{\Delta^2_{\mu_M}(x)}{4(\sigma_0^2+\sigma^2)} \right\} \geq C_2\Delta^2_{\mu_M}(x).
\]
Bringing together both pieces of information, we can take a common constant \(\tilde C = \min\{C_1, C_2\}/2\) and write
\[
  g_{0,\theta}(x) \geq \tilde C\left[\Delta^2_{\mu_M}(x) + (\sigma_0^2 - \sigma^2)^2\right].
\]
Then, integrating on both sides with respect to \(\qq_X\) yields
\[
  d_H^2(f_0, f_\theta)\geq \tilde C\left[\|\Delta_{\mu_M}\|^2_{2,\qq_X} + (\sigma_0^2 - \sigma^2)^2\right].
\]
To conclude, note that \(\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\geq (a+b)/\sqrt{2}\) for \(a,b\geq 0\), so we can take square roots on both sides and get the desired bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lemma:mean-square-lipschitz}]\label{proof:mean-square-lipschitz}
Since \(\E[X(t)]=0\), we have 
\[
  \E\left[(X(t) - X(s))^2\right] = K(t,t)-2K(t,s) + K(s,s).
\]
Because \(K\) is twice-continuously differentiable, the fundamental theorem of calculus and the symmetry of \(K\) give the integral representation
\[
  K(t,t)-2K(t,s) + K(s,s) = \int_s^t \int_s^t \partial_{uv}K(u, v)\, du\, dv.
\]
Let \(L := 1 + \sup_{(u, v) \in [0,1]^2} |\partial_{uv}K(u, v)|<\infty\). Then,
\[
  \E\left[(X(t) - X(s))^2\right] = \int_s^t \int_s^t \partial_{uv}K(u, v)\, du\, dv \leq  L(t-s)^2.
\]
\end{proof}
