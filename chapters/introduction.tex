\section{Functional data analysis}

\acf{FDA} has emerged as one of the most active areas of statistical research over the past decades, driven by the increasing availability of data collected over time, space, or other continuous domains. In contrast to classical multivariate statistics, where each data point is a vector, the \acs{FDA} framework treats each observation as a curve, surface, or more general function, typically viewed as a realization of an underlying stochastic process. While in principle the functional data could simply be regarded as a discretized vector in a very high dimension, there are often many advantages in taking into account its functional nature, ranging from modeling the correlation among points that are close in the domain, to extracting information that may be hidden in the derivatives of the function in question (see Figure~\ref{fig:tecator}). As a consequence, numerous proposals have arisen on how to suitably deal with functional data, which essentially explores statistical techniques to process, model and make inference on data varying over a continuum. The monograph by \citet{ramsay2005functional} is generally regarded as a standard reference for the subject, while detailed up-to-date accounts of the theory and applications can be found for example in \citet{hsing2015theoretical}, \citet{horvath2012inference}, or \citet{wang2016functional}.

This paradigm shift has enabled more nuanced analyses in diverse fields, including climatology, biomechanics, neuroimaging, and econometrics, where the fundamental units of observation are inherently functional~\citep{ullah2013applications}. Accordingly, there are many recent contributions on how to tackle functional data problems, both from a theoretical and practical standpoint. Chief among them is the approach of reducing the problem to a finite-dimensional one, for example using a truncated basis expansion or spline interpolation methods~\citep{muller2005generalized, aguilera2013comparative}. At the same time, much effort has been put into building a sound theoretical basis for \acs{FDA}, generalizing different frequentist concepts to the infinite-dimensional framework. Examples of this endeavor include the definition of centrality measures and depth-based notions for functional data~\citep{lopez2009concept}, functional analysis of variance tests~\citep{cuevas2004anova}, a functional Mahalanobis distance~\citep{galeano2015mahalanobis, berrendero2020mahalanobis}, or an extension of Fisher's discriminant analysis for random functions~\citep{shin2008extension}, among others.

As the name suggests, \acs{FDA} techniques are heavily inspired by functional analysis tools: Hilbert spaces, linear operators, orthonormal bases, and so on. Incidentally, a notion that also intersects with the classical theory of machine learning and pattern recognition, and that has attained popularity in recent years, is that of \acf{RKHSs} and their applications in functional data~\citep{kupresanin2010rkhs, yuan2010reproducing, berrendero2018use}.

On the other hand, Bayesian inference methods are ubiquitous in the realm of statistics, and though they also make use of random functions, the point of view is slightly different from the \acs{FDA} case. While there are recent works that handle functional data from a Bayesian perspective~\citep[e.g.][]{shi2011gaussian}, there is still no systematic approach to Bayesian methodologies within \acs{FDA}. It is precisely at this relatively unexplored intersection between \acs{FDA} and Bayesian methods that our work is aimed. We follow the path started by \citet{ferguson1974prior} of setting a prior distribution on a functional space and using the corresponding posterior for inference. In our case, we use a particular \acs{RKHS} as the ambient space, resulting in transdimensional mixture models that allow for a simple yet efficient Bayesian treatment of functional data. The posterior distribution can be approximated through computational methods, specifically via \acf{RJMCMC}.

Throughout this work we will consider a second-order stochastic process \(X=\{X(t): t\in[0,1]\}\) taking values in a certain space \(\mathcal X\), with mean function \(\mu(t)=\E[X(t)]\) and covariance function given by \(K(t,s)=\E[(X(t)-\mu(t))(X(s)-\mu(s))]\) for all \(t,s\in[0,1]\). The usual choice in the literature is \(\mathcal X=L^2[0,1]\), but we will suppose for convenience that the trajectories of \(X\) are continuous, so that \(\mathcal X=\mathcal C[0,1]\). In this way, pointwise evaluation is well-defined, which is necessary for the \acs{RKHS} models we propose. We will also assume that the covariance function \(K\) is strictly positive definite and continuous.

\subsection{Functional regression}\label{sec:functional-regression}

The problem of predicting a scalar response from a functional covariate is one that has gained traction over the last few decades, as more and more real-world data is being generated with an ever-increasing level of granularity in the measurements. Even though these problems can be formally stated with almost no differences from their finite-dimensional counterparts, there are some fundamental challenges as well as some subtle drawbacks that emerge as a result of working in infinite dimensions.

The most common scalar-on-function linear regression model is the classical \(L^2\)-model, widely popularized since the first edition (1997) of the pioneering monograph by~\citet{ramsay2005functional}. It can be seen as a generalization of the usual finite-dimensional model, replacing the scalar product in \(\R^D\) for that of the functional space \(L^2[0,1]\):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:l2-linear-model}
  Y = \alpha + \dotprod{X}{\beta}_2 + \epsilon = \alpha + \int_0^1 X(t)\beta(t)\, dt + \epsilon,
\end{equation}
where \(\alpha\in \R\), \(\epsilon\) is a random error term independent from \(X\) with \(\E [\epsilon]=0\), and the functional slope parameter \(\beta=\beta(\cdot)\) is a member of the infinite-dimensional space \(L^2[0, 1]\). In this case, the inference on \(\beta\) is hampered by the fact that \(L^2[0,1]\) is an extremely broad space that contains many nonsmooth or ill-behaved functions, so any estimation procedure involving optimization on it will typically be hard. In spite of this, model~\eqref{eq:l2-linear-model} is not flexible enough to include ``simple'' impact points submodels based on linear combinations of the marginals of \(X\), such as \(Y=\alpha + \beta_1 X(t_1)+ \cdots + \beta_p X(t_p) + \epsilon\) for some constants \(\alpha,\beta_j\in\R\) and instants \(t_j\in[0,1]\), which are especially appealing to practitioners confronted with functional data problems \citep[see][for additional details]{berrendero2024functional}. Moreover, the noninvertibility of the covariance operator associated with \(X\), which plays the role of the covariance matrix in the infinite case, invalidates the usual least squares theory \citep{cardot2010functional}. Thus, some regularization or dimensionality reduction technique is needed for parameter estimation; see \citet{reiss2017methods} for a summary of several widespread methods.

A similar \(L^2\)-based functional logistic equation can be derived for the binary classification problem via the logistic function
\begin{equation}\label{eq:l2-logistic-model}
  \pp(Y=1 \mid X) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\{-\alpha - \dotprod{X}{\beta}_2\}},
\end{equation}
where \(\alpha \in \R\) and \(\beta \in L^2[0, 1]\). In the equivalent finite-dimensional problem, the natural way of estimating the slope parameter is via its \acf{MLE}. However, apart from the issues outlined above for the linear model, functional settings pose an additional challenge in the logistic case: under fairly general conditions, the \acs{MLE} does not exist with probability one \citep[see][]{berrendero2023functional}.

\subsection{Functional hypothesis testing}

Another core problem in statistics involves hypothesis testing for quantities of interest. As such, considerable efforts have been devoted to generalizing classical testing frameworks to functional data, providing \(p\)-values and decision rules based on asymptotic approximations or bootstrap resampling. These methods include frequentist two-sample tests \citep{fan1998test,hall2007two,zhang2010two}, analysis of variance methods \citep{cuevas2004anova, cuesta2010simple, zhang2013analysis}, change point detection procedures \citep{berkes2009detecting,hormann2010weakly}, repeated measures analysis \citep{martinez2011repeated,smaga2020note} or dependence tests in linear regression \citep{cardot2003testing,kokoszka2008testing}, among others. However, the conceptually simpler one-sample tests, often used to detect the presence or absence of a certain effect within a given population, have received less attention in the \acs{FDA} literature. Notable exceptions include proposals by \citet{mas2007testing}, \citet{munk2008one} and \citet{zhang2013analysis}, where the authors explore different approaches to testing hypotheses about the mean of a functional sample.

On the other hand, Bayesian hypothesis testing methods---introduced in modern form by \citet{jeffreys1961theory}---are extensively studied and applied in multivariate settings, offering a principled alternative to the often criticized \(p\)-value approach \citep[see][and references therein]{robert2007bayesian, robert202250}. In particular, Bayesian model comparison provides a coherent framework for quantifying evidence in favor of competing hypotheses through the Bayes factor, defined as the ratio of their marginal likelihoods. Unlike a conventional significance test, a Bayes factor can provide evidence both for and against a null hypothesis, with posterior model probabilities additionally incorporating the prior odds of the competing hypotheses. These properties are particularly attractive when the objective is not merely to determine whether the observed data are inconsistent with a null hypothesis, but also to quantify the relative support for the null and alternative explanations. However, as in the functional regression case, the use of Bayesian testing methods in functional situations is less prominent, partly due to the challenges associated with infinite-dimensional parameter spaces, where the specification of prior distributions, the construction of suitable likelihoods, and the computation of posterior distributions require particular care.

\section{Essentials on reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces}

The Bayesian methodology proposed in this work relies heavily on the use of \acs{RKHSs}, so we will briefly outline the main characteristics of these spaces from a probabilistic point of view. For a more detailed account, we refer the reader to the monograph by \citet{berlinet2004reproducing}. 

To construct the \acs{RKHS} \(\Hcal(K)\) associated with the covariance function \(K\), we start by defining the functional space \(\Hcal_0(K)\) of all finite linear combinations of evaluations of \(K\), that is,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:h0}
  \Hcal_0(K) = \left\{ \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j K(t_j, \cdot): p \in \N,\, \beta_j \in \R,\, t_j \in [0, 1] \right\}.
\end{equation}
This space is endowed with the inner product 
\[
  \dotprod{f}{g}_K = \sum_{j, k} \beta_j \gamma_k K(t_j, s_k),
\]
given that \(f(\cdot)=\sum_j \beta_j K(t_j, \cdot) \) and \(g(\cdot)=\sum_k \gamma_k K(s_k, \cdot)\). Then, \(\Hcal(K)\) is defined to be the completion of \(\Hcal_0(K)\) under the norm induced by the scalar product \(\dotprod{\cdot}{\cdot}_K\). As it turns out, functions in this space satisfy the so-called \textit{reproducing property}: 
\[
  \dotprod{K(t, \cdot)}{f}_K = f(t),\quad \text{for all }\, f \in \Hcal(K) \text{ and } t \in [0, 1].
\]
An important consequence of this identity is that \(\Hcal(K)\) is a space of genuine functions and not of equivalence classes, since the values of the functions at specific time instants are in fact relevant, unlike in \(L^2\)-spaces.

Now, a particularly useful approach in statistics is to regard \(\Hcal(K)\) as an isometric copy of a well-known space related to \(X\). Specifically, via \textit{Loève's isometry} \citep{loeve1948fonctions} one can establish a congruence \(\Psi_X\) between \(\Hcal(K)\) and the closed linear span of the process, \(\bar{\mathcal L}(X)\), in the space of all random variables with finite second moment \citep[see Lemma 1.1 in][]{lukic2001stochastic}. This isometry is essentially the completion of the correspondence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loeves-isometry}
  \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j X(t_j) \longleftrightarrow \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j K(t_j, \cdot),
\end{equation}
and can be formally defined, in terms of its inverse, as 
\[
  \Psi^{-1}_X(U)(t) = \E[U X(t)]\quad \text{for } U \in \bar{\mathcal L}(X).
\]
Despite the close connection between the process \(X\) and the space \(\Hcal(K)\), special care must be taken when dealing with concrete realizations, since in general the trajectories of \(X\) do not belong to the corresponding \acs{RKHS} with probability one \citep[][Corollary~7.1]{lukic2001stochastic}. As a consequence, the expression \(\dotprod{x}{f}_K\) is ill-defined and lacks meaning when \(f\in\Hcal(K)\) and \(x\) is a realization of \(X\). However, following Parzen's approach in his seminal work \citep[][Theorem~4E]{parzen1961approach}, we can leverage Loève's isometry and identify \(\dotprod{X}{f}_K\) with the random variable in \(\bar{\mathcal L}(X)\) associated with \(f\in \Hcal(K)\), so that \(\dotprod{x}{f}_K \) with \(x=X(\omega)\) is defined as the image \(\Psi_x(f) := \Psi_X(f)(\omega)\). This notation, viewed as a formal extension of the inner product, often proves to be useful and convenient. We adopt this interpretation hereafter.

To accommodate a Bayesian framework for functional data, we propose simplified \acs{RKHS} models in which the functional parameter, say \(\beta\), is assumed to belong to \(\Hcal_0(K)\), i.e.,
\[
\beta(\cdot) = \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j K(t_j, \cdot),
\]
where \(\beta_j, t_j\) and \(p\) are unknown parameters that can be conveniently modeled through suitable prior distributions. We aim to exploit the \acs{RKHS} perspective to give a functional nature to the collection of finite-dimensional models based on random linear combinations of marginals (through Loève's isometry), offering a unified treatment of all the so-called \textit{impact points} models. These models have received considerable attention in the functional literature, due to their simplicity and practical relevance \citep[see][]{kneip2016functional,poss2020superconsistent,mckeague2010fractals, lindquist2009logistic}.


\section{Posterior consistency}\label{sec:posterior-consistency-intro}

A major aim of this work is to study the basic theoretical question of posterior consistency within the proposed Bayesian framework. Consistency and posterior concentration are a type of frequentist validation criteria that have arguably been an active point of research in the last few decades, particularly in infinite-dimensional settings. To put it simply, posterior consistency ensures that with enough data points, the Bayesian updating mechanism works as intended and the posterior distribution eventually concentrates around the true value of the parameters, supposing the model is well specified. For an in-depth treatment of posterior consistency and related concepts, we refer the reader to the highly relevant work of \citet{ghosal2017fundamentals}.

Consider the general setting of an i.i.d.\ sample \(X_1,\dots, X_n\) from \(X\). Let us fix a prior distribution \(\Pi\) for random variables \(\theta\) on the parameter space \(\Theta\), that is, \(\theta \sim \Pi\), and let \(\pp_\theta\) represent a sampling model (a distribution on \(\mathcal X\) indexed by \(\theta \in \Theta\)) such that \(X | \theta \sim \pp_{\theta}\). Furthermore, assume that the model is well-specified, i.e., there is a true value \(\theta_{0}\in\Theta\) such that \(X \sim \pp_{\theta_0}\), and denote by \(\pp_0^\infty\) and \(\pp_0^{(n)}\) the joint probability measures of \((X_1, X_2, \dots)\) and \((X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n)\), respectively, when \(\theta_0\) is the true value of the parameter.

\begin{definition} We say that the posterior distribution is (strongly) consistent at \(\theta_0\) if for every neighborhood \(B\) of \(\theta_0\) we have
  \[
    \lim_{n\to\infty} \Pi_n(\theta \in B \mid X_1, \dots, X_n) = 1 \quad \pp_0^\infty-\text{a.s.}
  \]
\end{definition}

Note that the conditional probabilities are computed under the assumed joint distribution of \((\theta, (X_1, X_2,\dots))\). Essentially, we are saying that the posterior concentrates around \(\theta_0\) for almost all sequences of data, and thus the effect of the prior gets diluted as more and more data is available for the inference. Furthermore, when the parameter space carries a metric, say \(d\), we are also interested in the speed at which the posterior approaches the true parameter \(\theta_0\). This is what we call a posterior contraction rate, which is a significant refinement of the comparatively weaker concept of consistency.

\begin{definition}
  A sequence \(\epsilon_n\to 0\) is a posterior contraction rate at \(\theta_0\) with respect to \(d\) if \(\Pi_n(\theta: d(\theta_0, \theta) \geq M_n \epsilon_n | X_1,\dots, X_n) \to 0\) in \(\pp_0^{(n)}\)-probability, for every \(M_n\to\infty\).
\end{definition}

Naturally, once we have established a contraction rate, every slower sequence is also a valid contraction rate. Even though we are interested in the fastest possible rate, this is often difficult to compute or does not exist at all. Hence, we are usually content with finding a good enough rate, which we call ``the'' rate of contraction for the model.

It turns out that, under very general conditions, the posterior distribution is always consistent at almost every value of \(\theta_0\) with respect to the measure induced by the prior \citep{doob1949application}. First, we need the underlying spaces to be sufficiently nice.

\begin{definition}
  A Polish space is a separable, completely metrizable topological space; that is, a space homeomorphic to a complete metric space that has a countable dense subset.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Doob's consistency theorem]\label{th:doob}
  Let the path space \(\mathcal X\) and the parameter space \(\Theta\) be Polish spaces, endowed with their respective Borel sigma-algebras. If \(\theta \mapsto \pp_\theta\) is one-to-one and \(\theta \mapsto \pp_\theta(A)\) is measurable for all measurable sets \(A\subseteq \mathcal X\), then the posterior distribution is consistent at \(\Pi\)-almost all values of \(\Theta\). That is, there exists \(\Theta_*\subseteq \Theta\) such that \(\Pi(\Theta_*)=1\) and for all \(\theta_0\in\Theta_*\), if \(X_1,X_2,\ldots \sim \pp_{\theta_0}\) i.i.d., then for any neighborhood \(B\) of \(\theta_0\) we have
  \[
    \lim_{n\to\infty} \Pi_n(\theta \in B \mid X_1,\dots, X_n) = 1 \quad \pp_{0}^\infty-\text{a.s.}
  \]
  Moreover, for any \(g\in L^1(\Pi)\) we have
  \[
  \lim_{n\to\infty} \E[g(\theta)\mid X_1,\dots, X_n] = g(\theta_0) \quad \pp_0^\infty-\text{a.s.}
  \]
\end{theorem}

A fairly comprehensive analysis of this result is available in \citet{miller2018detailed}, and an extension to finite mixture models is studied in \citet{miller2023consistency}. We draw inspiration from this latter work to establish consistency results in the models we propose.

In a nonparametric setting where the object of interest is a probability density, there is a stronger consistency result by \citet{schwartz1965bayes} which omits the \(\Pi\)-almost sure qualification under some more restrictive conditions, though it requires a dominated model with a reference measure \(\rho\) and a density \(f_\theta\) to estimate. Consider a class of densities \(\mathcal F=\{f_\theta: \theta\in\Theta\}\), a prior distribution \(\Pi_{\mathcal F}\) on \(\mathcal F\), and suppose that there is a ``true'' density \(f_0\in\mathcal F\). We introduce below a few concepts that lie at the core of most extensions of Schwartz's consistency theorem. 

Let \(\theta, \tilde\theta \in \Theta\). Recall that the Kullback--Leibler divergence from \(f_\theta\) to \(f_{\tilde\theta}\) is defined as 
\[
  \dkl(f_\theta \,\|\, f_{\tilde\theta}) = \bigintssss f_\theta \log (f_\theta/f_{\tilde\theta})\, d\rho.
\] 
We say that \(f_0\) belongs to the \textit{Kullback--Leibler support of \(\Pi_{\mathcal F}\)}, and write it as \(f_0 \in \kl(\Pi_{\mathcal F})\), if \(\Pi_{\mathcal F}(f_\theta: \dkl(f_0 \,\|\, f_\theta) < \epsilon) > 0\) for all \(\epsilon > 0\). This can be interpreted as saying that the prior places sufficient mass ``near'' \(f_0\).

On the other hand, let \(N(\epsilon, \mathcal F, d)\) denote the \(\epsilon\)-covering number of the set \(\mathcal F\) with respect to the distance \(d\), i.e., the minimal number of \(d\)-balls of radius \(\epsilon\) needed to cover \(\mathcal F\). 

Finally, define the Hellinger distance \(d_H\) between two densities as
\[
  d^2_H(f_\theta, f_{\tilde\theta}) = 1 - \bigintssss \sqrt{f_\theta f_{\tilde\theta}}\, d\rho.
\] 

The following result gives consistency in the sense of Schwartz, by means of a sieve that controls the complexity of the model as the sample size \(n\) increases, measured in terms of the \textit{metric entropy} (the logarithm of the \(\epsilon\)-covering number) of the model.

\begin{theorem}[Theorem 6.23 in \citealp{ghosal2017fundamentals}]\label{th:consistency-hellinger}
  Suppose that for every \(\epsilon > 0\) there exist measurable sets \(\mathcal F_n \subseteq \mathcal F\) and a constant \(C>0\) such that, for sufficiently large \(n\): 
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item \(\log N(\epsilon, \mathcal F_n, d_H) \leq n\epsilon^2\).
    \item \(\Pi_{\mathcal F}(\mathcal F \setminus \mathcal F_n) \leq \exp\{-Cn\}\).
  \end{enumerate}
  Then the posterior distribution is consistent relative to \(d_H\) at every density \(f_0\in \kl(\Pi_{\mathcal F})\).
\end{theorem}

To obtain posterior contraction rates, there is an extension of Theorem~\ref{th:consistency-hellinger} that employs the so-called \textit{second Kullback--Leibler variation}, defined as 
\[
  V_2(f_0, f_\theta)=\E_{f_0}\left[\left(\log(f_0/f_\theta) - \dkl(f_0 \,\|\, f_\theta)\right)^2\right],
\]
to quantify the Kullback--Leibler property of \(f_0\) in terms of convergence speed. In particular, for \(\epsilon>0\) consider the neighborhood
\[
B_2(f_0, \epsilon) = \left\{f\in \mathcal F: \dkl(f_0 \,\|\, f_\theta) < \epsilon^2,\  V_2(f_0, f_\theta) < \epsilon^2\right\}.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Theorem~8.9 in \citealp{ghosal2017fundamentals}]\label{th:contraction-rate}
  Suppose that there exist measurable sets \(\mathcal{F}_n \subseteq \mathcal F\) and a constant \(C>0\) such that, for a sequence \(\epsilon_n \to 0\) with \(n\epsilon_n^2 \to \infty\), the following hold for sufficiently large \(n\):
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item \( \log N(\epsilon_n/2, \mathcal{F}_n, d_H) \leq n \epsilon_n^2\).
    \item \( \Pi_{\mathcal F}(\mathcal{F} \setminus \mathcal{F}_n) \leq \exp\{-(C+4)n \epsilon_n^2\}\).
    \item \( \Pi_{\mathcal F}(B_2(f_0, \epsilon_n)) \geq \exp\{-C n\epsilon_n^2\}\).
  \end{enumerate}
  Then the posterior rate of contraction at \(f_0 \) with respect to \(d_H\) is \(\epsilon_n \).
\end{theorem}

Note that the condition \(n\epsilon_n^2\to\infty\) excludes the parametric rate \(n^{-1/2}\), since in nonparametric situations the rates are usually slower. Condition (i) bounds the complexity of the model, condition (ii) merely expresses that subsets of negligible prior mass do not play a role in the rate of contraction, and condition (iii) ensures that sufficient prior mass is put ``near'' the true density \(f_0\) at an appropriate rate.

Lastly, we are also interested in evaluating posterior consistency in a testing situation, where consistency means that, as the sample size grows, the posterior probability assigned to the true hypothesis converges in probability to one (under the true data generating distribution). To obtain contraction rates we can also use the standard concept of \textit{stochastic boundedness} \citep[e.g.][Chapter 2]{vaart1998asymptotic}.

\begin{definition}\label{def:stochastic-boundedness}
  Given a sequence of nonnegative random variables \(Y_n\), whose distribution is determined by \(\pp\), and a sequence of positive numbers \(a_n\), we write \(Y_n=O_{\pp}(a_n)\) if for any \(\epsilon>0\) there exist \(N,M>0\) such that \(\pp(Y_n/a_n > M) < \epsilon\) for all \(n> N\). In this case, if \(a_n\to 0\) we say that \(a_n\) is a contraction rate for \(Y_n\), or equivalently, that \(Y_n\) decays at rate \(a_n\) (in probability).
\end{definition}

\section{Contributions and structure of the thesis}

As we established before, we are concerned with functional statistical problems, where the term ``functional'' refers to the fact that both the sample data and the target parameters are real functions defined on compact intervals. More specifically, we are interested in the use of a Bayesian paradigm for inference in these problems, due to the relative shortage of Bayesian methodologies in functional data. Indeed, one major hurdle in these approaches is the intrinsic difficulty of properly handling the prior distribution and computing the corresponding posterior in function spaces.

Our proposal essentially relies on the use of the \acs{RKHS} associated with the covariance function of the underlying stochastic process, viewed as a convenient ambient space to define and calculate the relevant prior and posterior distributions. In this context, we develop a unified theoretical and computational framework for two major statistical problems: functional regression and functional hypothesis testing. We summarize below the main contributions of this work.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Using a simplified \acs{RKHS} framework, we propose linear and logistic regression models with functional regressors and scalar output, presented as alternatives to the unrestricted complexity of the usual models based on \(L^2\)-spaces. Following a similar strategy, we also propose a one-sample test for the mean function of a Gaussian process, testing an exact point-null hypothesis against a general functional alternative.
  \item We consider a Bayesian approach for inference in both functional problems, facilitated by regarding the functional parameters as members of the corresponding \acs{RKHS}.
  \item In both situations, a comprehensive asymptotic theory that validates the Bayesian inference is provided, establishing posterior consistency (in two different senses) and near-optimal posterior contraction rates.
  \item A computational Python implementation of the proposed methods is also given, based on an efficient \acs{RJMCMC} sampler for posterior approximation.
  \item Lastly, we provide empirical studies and real data applications, which show that our methods are competitive against existing frequentist alternatives.
\end{itemize}

The research presented in this thesis has resulted in two articles. The Bayesian functional regression models are contained in \citet{berrendero2025bayesian}, published in \textit{Bayesian Analysis}. The Bayesian functional test is developed in \citet{berrendero2026bayesian}, currently submitted for publication.

\subsubsection*{Structure}

The rest of this document is organized as follows.

In \slsc{Chapter~\ref{ch:functional-regression}} we propose two Bayesian models for functional linear and logistic regression, enabled by approximating the infinite-dimensional functional parameter with a finite linear combination of kernel sections. This representation leads naturally to models with an unknown number of components and unknown impact points, for which suitable prior distributions are considered. We then introduce \acs{RJMCMC} as the main computational tool and develop four prediction strategies, combining posterior predictive inference with Bayesian variable selection. The theoretical part of the chapter establishes posterior consistency for the proposed models, first through an approach based on Doob's theorem and then through Schwartz-type arguments leading to posterior contraction rates. Lastly, the proposed methodology is assessed through extensive simulation studies and applications to real functional data, with comparisons against a range of established functional and finite-dimensional methods, followed by a discussion of computational performance and practical model validation.

\slsc{Chapter~\ref{ch:functional-testing}} extends the Bayesian \acs{RKHS} framework to functional hypothesis testing, focusing on a one-sample test for the mean function of a Gaussian process. We consider a point-null hypothesis and introduce a spike-and-slab prior that assigns positive probability to the null while retaining a continuous alternative. The resulting posterior distribution is simplified by analytically marginalizing the coefficients, after which \acs{RJMCMC} is used to explore the remaining variable-dimensional parameter space. In addition to posterior null probabilities and Bayes factors, the framework provides posterior summaries of the mean function and simultaneous credible bands, thereby combining hypothesis testing with uncertainty quantification. We then address the practical case of an unknown covariance function through a plug-in strategy based on regularized covariance estimators, and establish consistency and contraction results both for known covariance and for the plug-in setting. We conclude with simulation studies and a real-data application, illustrating the empirical performance and interpretability of the proposed test.

In \slsc{Chapter~\ref{ch:rjmcmc}} we take a closer look at the computational tools used to approximate the posterior distribution in the proposed Bayesian models. We first introduce the \acs{RJMCMC} framework from a theoretical perspective, and then we describe the implementation details of the Python library \slsc{Eryn} \citep{karnesis2023eryn}. We conclude with a discussion of the label switching phenomenon, which is a common issue in the Bayesian analysis of finite mixture models.

Finally, in \slsc{Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions}} we summarize the main findings and discuss future work and open lines of research.

We provide several appendices that complement the main text. In \slsc{Appendix~\ref{app:comparison-test}} we review several existing techniques for performing one-sample tests with functional data; in \slsc{Appendix~\ref{app:tables-bfr}} we provide the full tables of numerical results for the functional regression experiments; and in \slsc{Appendix~\ref{app:source-code}} we briefly comment on the source code for the computational implementation we developed.
